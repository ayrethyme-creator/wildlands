! THE BADGE MEMBER CARDS - one per MEMBERSHIP, not one per species.
!
! Ayr, 2026-08-29: "Not every species in a genus qualifies for the badge, and that's
! why an individual species had to be assigned. These concepts actually do need to be
! spelled out and explained somewhere, which is really what the badge system is for -
! it's not just a title with names."
!
! Format:  Badge :: Species :: the card text
!
! One line per line of BADGES.txt membership. A species in four badges has four
! cards here, because it is being claimed for four different reasons. Uncle Albert
! checks every card names a real membership, reports coverage, and fails on a card
! for a membership that does not exist.
!
! 430 memberships across 324 species. This file is the work queue for all of them.
!
! ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
! HOW TO WRITE ONE
! ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
! The card answers ONE question: why THIS species and not its cousin? It is not a
! second field-guide entry and it must not repeat the animal's own card - the reader
! got here from that card, or is about to go to it by the link.
!
! Say the mechanism. "It changes sex" is the badge title. "Born male, and when the
! female dies the dominant male becomes her" is the card.
!
! Every sentence here is a checkable claim about biology, so The Librarian reads
! this file. A wrong card is an error in the educational core, not a typo.
!
! ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
! PAIR BADGES NAME THEIR PARTNER - Ayr, 2026-08-29
! ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
! Where a badge is built of pairs or of opposing directions, the card SAYS WHICH
! ONE it is paired with or which direction it runs. Otherwise a player can hold four
! members of "Same Answer Twice" and have demonstrated nothing - a sugar glider and
! a thylacine are two marsupials, not a convergence.
!
! The badges this applies to: Same Answer Twice (pairs), The Switch (two opposite
! directions), The Island Rule (dwarfism AND gigantism).
!
! ============================================================================
! THE FIRST THREE, written as the worked examples in GDD s12
! ============================================================================
The Switch :: Percula Clownfish :: It runs the OPPOSITE WAY to the wrasses in this badge. A clownfish is born male. The largest fish in an anemone is the breeding female and the second largest is her mate; when she dies, HE BECOMES HER, and the next male up moves into his place. Protandry - male first. The parrotfish and the wrasses beside it in this badge go female first, and the badge holds both.
The Switch :: Stoplight Parrotfish :: Born female, and some later become male - protogyny, the opposite direction to the clownfish in this same badge. The change is not only reproductive: the terminal-phase male is so differently coloured from the female that the two were originally described as SEPARATE SPECIES, which is where the name comes from.
Same Answer Twice :: Sugar Glider :: Paired with the FLYING SQUIRREL in this badge, and the pairing is the point. One is a marsupial, one is a placental mammal, and their lineages separated well over a hundred million years ago. Neither inherited gliding from the other - both arrived at the same membrane between wrist and ankle because the problem was the same one.

! --- The Granny. Menopause is known in six species: us and five toothed whales.
The Granny :: Orca :: The species menopause was found in first, and the one the grandmother hypothesis was actually tested on. Females stop calving around forty and can live past eighty, so a third of a long life is spent not breeding at all. The old females lead the group to salmon in poor years, and when a mother dies her grown son's chance of dying inside the following year rises several times over. She is worth more to the pod as a guide than as a mother, which is the whole argument.
The Granny :: Beluga :: Confirmed here in 2018, and confirmed by counting scars. Every ovulation leaves a mark in the ovary, so a dead female carries a tally of her own reproductive life — and in old belugas that tally stops rising long before the animal does. Beluga and narwhal were added together by that one study, taking the number of species known to do this from three to five.
The Granny :: Narwhal :: Added by the same 2018 study as the beluga, and the two are each other's closest relatives — the only living members of the family Monodontidae. That matters for the badge: menopause is not one oddity repeated by accident, it turns up in a family of two AND in the dolphins beside them, which is what makes it look like an answer to a shared problem.
The Granny :: Short-finned Pilot Whale :: The second species it was ever found in, decades before the beluga and the narwhal joined the list, and the clearest demonstration of what the extra years are for. Old females go on producing milk for years after their own last calf and nurse calves that are not theirs — the grandmother is not a metaphor here, she is doing the feeding.
The Granny :: False Killer Whale :: The fifth, and it is not a killer whale — it is a large dolphin named for a resemblance in the skull. That is the point of its place here: menopause in whales is not a killer-whale peculiarity that got copied. It shows up across the dolphin family, in animals that live in tight lifelong groups, which is the condition the grandmother hypothesis actually needs.

! --- Blood Queen. Menstruation is a catarrhine trait, so every member here is an
! --- Old World monkey or an ape - and each card has to say something the reader
! --- could not get from the one beside it.
Blood Queen :: Gorilla :: It menstruates and you would almost never know. The flow is so slight it is usually invisible even to keepers who see the animal every day, and the cycle runs about a month. Nothing advertises it — no swelling, no colour — which is worth knowing beside the baboons and chimpanzees in this badge, whose cycles are impossible to miss.
Blood Queen :: Chimpanzee :: The bleeding is not the signal here. What the troop reads is the sexual swelling: the skin around the rump inflates over days, peaks near ovulation, and subsides. A female at maximum swelling is the centre of the group's attention, so in chimpanzees the visible part of the cycle is fertility being announced rather than a period being had.
Blood Queen :: Bonobo :: The cycle is there and it has been decoupled from fertility. Bonobo swellings last far longer than the fertile window and females are sexually active through most of the cycle and while pregnant, so sex stops being a reliable signal of when she can conceive. That is the difference from the chimpanzee beside it in this badge, and it is thought to be part of why bonobo groups settle conflict the way they do.
Blood Queen :: Olive Baboon :: The most legible cycle in the badge. The swelling is enormous, changes colour, and its size tracks the chances of conception closely enough that males rank their attention by it. Because these troops have been watched continuously for decades, the olive baboon is where much of what is known about how cycles interact with rank and alliance was worked out.
Blood Queen :: Japanese Macaque :: The only member of this badge that stops. It is a seasonal breeder at the northern edge of primate range, so females cycle only through autumn and winter and are simply not cycling for much of the year. Menstruation in a monkey living in snow is on a calendar rather than a rolling loop.
Blood Queen :: Sumatran Orangutan :: It has the longest gap between births of any mammal — eight or nine years — so a female spends most of her adult life pregnant or nursing and cycles only in the narrow windows between. It is the rarest menstruation in the badge, not because the animal is unusual internally but because it almost never gets the chance.
Blood Queen :: Mandrill :: The cycle is written on the male as well as the female. Female swelling tracks the cycle in the ordinary catarrhine way; what is unusual is that the male's blue and red face and rump brighten and fade with his own rank and hormones, so a mandrill troop signals reproductive state in both directions at once.
Blood Queen :: Gelada :: It moved the signal. Geladas sit on their haunches on cliff grassland for hours to graze, which makes a rump display useless — so the swelling is on the CHEST instead, a patch of bare skin that blisters into a ring of fluid-filled beads through the cycle. Same information, relocated because of how the animal sits.
Blood Queen :: Vervet Monkey :: A small, common, much-studied African monkey with a quiet cycle: it menstruates without the conspicuous swelling of the baboons and chimpanzees around it. Its place in the badge is to keep the reader from concluding that menstruation and a swollen rump are the same thing. They are not, and most of the 1.5% do it without any display at all.

! --- No Father Required. Parthenogenesis in vertebrates.
No Father Required :: Komodo Dragon :: The case that made the newspapers, and it happened twice in European zoos in 2006 in females that had never met a male. It also has a strange consequence: in this system the mother can only produce SONS, because of how the sex chromosomes recombine. A lone female washed onto an island could therefore found a population by producing sons and then breeding with them.
No Father Required :: Zebra Shark :: The one that proves it is not a captivity artefact and not a last resort. A female called Leonie at Reef HQ in Australia bred normally with a male for years, was separated from him, and then began producing viable pups on her own — the first vertebrate recorded switching from sexual to asexual reproduction with a live mate history behind her.
No Father Required :: Ball Python :: A snake in which it has been confirmed genetically rather than assumed from an isolated female — the offspring carried only the mother's markers. It matters that this species is bred by the thousand by keepers, because that is why it was noticed at all: enough animals under enough observation for a rare event to be caught and then checked.
No Father Required :: California Condor :: Found in 2021, and found by looking backwards. Routine genetic checks on a captive-breeding programme turned up two chicks with no father's DNA in them — hatched years earlier, from females who had fertile males available at the time. Nobody had suspected it, and it was only visible because the programme had kept genetic records on every bird for decades.
No Father Required :: Smalltooth Sawfish :: The first vertebrate ever shown to be doing this IN THE WILD rather than in a tank. Routine genetic sampling of a Florida population found about three percent of the animals had no father. That population is critically reduced and scattered, which is the uncomfortable reading: a female may be reproducing alone because there is no longer a male to find.
No Father Required :: Green Anaconda :: Recorded in females kept alone for years, producing litters that were genetically half-clones of the mother. It belongs in this badge next to the ball python for contrast — the same phenomenon in two very distantly related snakes, which says the machinery is old rather than a quirk one lineage invented.

! --- The Switch. Sequential hermaphroditism. Two of these were written as the
! --- worked examples in GDD s12 and are above.
The Switch :: Bluestreak Cleaner Wrasse :: Protogyny, female first, running the OPPOSITE way to the clownfish in this badge. A cleaning station is held by one male and a harem of females; when he goes, the largest female changes. The change begins within HOURS — she starts behaving as a male the same day — and the body follows over a week or two. Behaviour leads, anatomy catches up.
The Switch :: Goliath Grouper :: Female first, and it is the reason this badge is not only a curiosity. Groupers gather in spawning aggregations, the biggest fish in them are the males, and a fishery that takes the biggest fish takes the males specifically. The species was fished to near-collapse and protected in 1990; the recovery had to wait for females to grow large enough to become the males that had been removed.
The Switch :: Emperor Angelfish :: Female first, in a harem held by one male — and the juvenile is so unlike the adult, dark blue with white rings, that the two were long taken for separate species. So this fish changes its appearance twice in a life: once growing up, and once changing sex.
The Switch :: Six-Line Wrasse :: The small end of the same wrasse pattern as the cleaner wrasse beside it: female first, with the dominant female of a group becoming male when the male is gone. It is here because it is a reef fish people keep in tanks, which means the change is something an aquarist can watch happen in a living room over a fortnight.
The Switch :: Humphead Wrasse :: Female first, and the largest fish in this badge — over two metres. That size is the problem. It changes sex late in life, so the breeding males are the oldest and biggest animals on the reef, and those are exactly the ones taken for the live-fish restaurant trade. Removing large individuals removes the males faster than a headcount of the species suggests.

! --- Dad's Turn. Male pregnancy. Three members, and Ayr ruled on 2026-08-29 that
! --- it stands at three rather than invent a fourth.
Dad's Turn :: Pygmy Seahorse :: The male has a sealed pouch on the belly. The female deposits eggs into it, he fertilises them inside, and then carries them — supplying oxygen, controlling the salt balance of the fluid around them, and giving birth to live young at the end. This is not a father minding eggs. The embryos are inside his body and he is running their environment.
Dad's Turn :: Weedy Seadragon :: No pouch at all. The male carries the eggs in the open, on a patch of spongy skin under his tail that softens and forms cups for them at breeding time, each egg sitting in its own hollow. Same job as the seahorse in this badge, done without the equipment — which is what makes the pair worth holding together.
Dad's Turn :: Leafy Seadragon :: The same open brood patch as the weedy seadragon, on an animal so heavily fringed that the eggs under its tail are one more thing lost in the outline. The two seadragons and the seahorse are close relatives, all in the family Syngnathidae, and that family is the only place in the vertebrate world where pregnancy is the male's job.

! --- On Pause. Embryonic diapause - the embryo stops and waits.
On Pause :: Stoat :: The extreme case, and the one the badge is named around. A female mates in summer and gives birth the following spring, nearly ten months later — but the embryo is only growing for about four weeks of that. The rest is a blastocyst floating unattached in the uterus, doing nothing. Female stoats are also mated as blind, unweaned kits in the nest, and carry that paused embryo for the best part of a year before it starts.
On Pause :: Fisher :: Almost a year of pause. It mates within days of giving birth, and the new embryo then waits about ten months before implanting, so a female is technically pregnant nearly all the time while actually gestating for around fifty days. It lets a solitary animal that meets a mate rarely put the birth where the food is instead of where the meeting was.
On Pause :: Black Bear :: The pause is what makes the winter den work. Mating is in early summer and the embryo waits until November — and if she has not laid down enough fat by then, it never implants at all and the pregnancy simply ends. The decision to have cubs is taken in autumn, by her body, on the evidence of the year she has had.
On Pause :: Sea Otter :: A carnivore that pauses for two to three months in water rather than for a winter. It has no blubber and no den; it survives on the densest fur of any animal and on eating a quarter of its body weight a day. Delaying implantation puts the single pup into the part of the year the mother can actually afford it.
On Pause :: Red Kangaroo :: The most elaborate version anywhere. A female can hold a paused embryo, a joey in the pouch on the teat, and a young-at-foot all at once — and she supplies the two young with different milk from different teats at the same time. When the pouch young leaves or dies, the paused embryo restarts. In drought the whole queue simply stops.
On Pause :: Nine-banded Armadillo :: Diapause plus something rarer. It delays implantation for three to four months, and then the single embryo splits into four — so it gives birth to identical quadruplets, always four, always the same sex. It is why this animal is used in leprosy research: four genetically identical subjects at a time.
On Pause :: American Mink :: Pause with a complication. A female can mate with several males over a fortnight, and embryos from different fathers can all sit paused and then implant together, so one litter has more than one father. The delay is short, a few weeks, but it is long enough to keep the mating season open after conception.
On Pause :: Wolverine :: It mates in summer and implants in winter, and the implantation itself depends on how much carrion she has found — a female in a poor year does not carry through. It is a low-density animal in a hard landscape, and this is how a chance meeting in July can still become cubs in a snow den in February.
On Pause :: Pine Marten :: Six or seven months of pause for about a month of actual gestation, which for a long time made the species look impossible to breed: keepers recorded a mating and then nothing for most of a year. The badge's point is here — a pregnancy that shows no sign of existing is easy to mistake for a failed one.
On Pause :: Red-necked Wallaby :: The macropod system running on a different clock from the red kangaroo beside it. In this species the pause is seasonal rather than triggered by drought: births are timed so that the young leave the pouch in spring. Same mechanism, tuned to a temperate year instead of an unpredictable one.
On Pause :: European Badger :: The longest wait in the badge. Badgers mate at almost any time of year and implantation happens in December for all of them, so an embryo conceived in February can sit paused for ten months. It means a sett's cubs are all born within a few weeks of each other regardless of when the matings happened.

! --- One Shot. Semelparity - breed once, then die.
One Shot :: Sockeye Salmon :: It stops eating when it enters fresh water and burns its own body to get upriver, absorbing its stomach, changing shape and turning red on the way. Everything it does after that is paid for out of tissue it will not replace. The dying is not a side effect: the carcasses carry ocean nitrogen hundreds of kilometres inland, and the forest along the river is measurably built out of them.
One Shot :: Giant Pacific Octopus :: The female lays up to a hundred thousand eggs, hangs them in a den and then never leaves. She blows water over them and grooms them for six months or more, eats nothing at all, and dies at about the time they hatch. Her body is what the vigil costs. Nothing is retained for a second attempt.
One Shot :: Atlas Moth :: It has no functioning mouth. The adult emerges without a working proboscis, cannot feed, and lives about a week on fat stored while it was a caterpillar — so the entire adult stage exists to find a mate. Everything that looks like a lifetime happened before the wings.
One Shot :: Seventeen-year Cicada :: Seventeen years underground drinking root sap, then a few weeks above ground, then dead. The number is the interesting part: thirteen and seventeen are both prime, so a predator on a two-, three- or four-year cycle can never line its own boom up with the emergence. Breeding once is what makes the timing possible at all.
One Shot :: Sea Lamprey :: The adult stops feeding once it starts upstream, builds a nest of stones with its sucker mouth, spawns and dies. It also has the longer half of the story behind it — years as a blind, toothless, filter-feeding larva buried in silt before it ever becomes the animal people recognise.
One Shot :: Luna Moth :: The same mouthless week as the Atlas moth in this badge, at a quarter of the size and in North American woodland. It is here as the pair to it: two unrelated giant silk moths that independently gave up eating as adults, which says the trade — no feeding, no second chance, all of it spent on finding one mate — is a strategy rather than an accident of one lineage.

! --- The Colony. Eusociality.
The Colony :: Naked Mole-Rat :: The only mammal in the room, and it meets the full definition rather than an approximation: one breeding queen, non-breeding workers who dig and forage, overlapping generations, and lifelong division of labour. The queen is not born one — a female who becomes queen LENGTHENS HER OWN SPINE, growing the vertebrae apart to carry more pups, which no other mammal does.
The Colony :: Western Honeybee :: The colony everyone thinks they know, and the part usually missed is that the workers decide. They choose which larvae are fed royal jelly and therefore which become queens, they vote on a new nest site by dancing, and they will replace a failing queen. The queen lays; she does not rule.
The Colony :: Leafcutter Ant :: Eusociality plus agriculture. They do not eat the leaves they carry — they compost them to grow a fungus that exists nowhere else, in gardens tended by the smallest workers, protected with antibiotics from bacteria living on the ants' own bodies. One colony can hold millions of individuals across several castes with physically different bodies for different jobs.
The Colony :: Honeypot Ant :: The caste that is a container. Selected workers are fed until their abdomens swell to the size of a grape and they can no longer walk; they hang from the ceiling of the nest for months as living stores, and regurgitate on demand when the colony is short. A eusocial society can afford to turn an individual into furniture.
The Colony :: Buff-tailed Bumblebee :: The annual version, and it is the useful contrast to the honeybee beside it. A bumblebee colony is founded from nothing each spring by one mated queen, grows through the summer, produces new queens and males, and then dies entirely — every worker and the old queen with it. Only the new queens survive the winter, alone.
The Colony :: Pistol Shrimp :: Some snapping shrimp are the only eusocial animals in the sea. Colonies of hundreds live inside a single sponge with one breeding queen and non-breeding defenders who use the snapping claw on intruders. It was found in the 1990s and it is why this badge is not simply a list of insects: the same social answer turned up independently underwater, in a crustacean.

! --- Til Death. Lifelong monogamy.
Til Death :: Wandering Albatross :: Pairs stay together for decades and divorce is rare — but the courtship costs years first. A young bird spends several seasons dancing with many partners before settling, and the pair then meet at the same nest site after months apart at opposite ends of an ocean. With one chick every two years, there is no time to spend on a partner who cannot do it.
Til Death :: Rosy-faced Lovebird :: The name is not sentiment. A pair sits in contact for hours, feeds each other beak to beak, and a bird separated from its mate visibly deteriorates — which is why keepers are told not to house one alone. The bond is maintained by constant physical contact rather than by a display, and it holds outside the breeding season.
Til Death :: Mute Swan :: Pairs usually hold for life and hold a territory together, which is most of the point: two birds defending one stretch of water year after year do better than either would alone. Divorce does happen after a failed season, which is worth saying plainly — the folklore says never, and the ringing data says rarely.
Til Death :: Grey Wolf :: The pack is a family: a bonded pair and their offspring of several years, not a gang of rivals with a dominance ladder. That correction matters, because the "alpha wolf" idea came from unrelated captive animals thrown together and has been retracted by the researcher who popularised it. In the wild the alpha is just the mother or the father.
Til Death :: Siamang :: A gibbon that sings to hold the pair together. The male and female perform a loud coordinated duet, with the throat sac inflating to a size that carries the sound kilometres through forest, and the pattern of the duet is particular to that pair. It advertises the bond and the territory in one performance.
Til Death :: Bald Eagle :: Pairs return to the same nest year after year and add to it, so the structure grows for decades — the largest recorded weighed over a tonne and eventually brought the tree down. The nest is the relationship made physical: neither bird could build or hold it alone, and rebuilding from nothing with a new partner would cost years.
Til Death :: Red-crowned Crane :: The pair bond is the dance. Cranes leap, bow, throw vegetation and call in unison, and the unison call is sexually specific — the male and female produce different notes that interlock into one sound. It is done in courtship and repeated for years afterwards, which is the tell: this is bond maintenance, not just mate attraction.
Til Death :: Atlantic Puffin :: The pair is loyal to the burrow as much as to each other. Both birds return to the same hole on the same cliff after a winter spent separately far out at sea, and the reunion is what re-forms the pair. Fidelity here is a consequence of site fidelity, which is a different route to the same badge.
Til Death :: Indri :: The largest living lemur, pair-bonded for life, and it sings — a wailing duet that carries kilometres through Madagascan forest and is used to hold the family's territory. It has also never been kept alive in captivity for long, so a pair separated by forest clearance cannot simply be re-housed and paired again elsewhere.

! --- The Sixth Sense. Electroreception.
The Sixth Sense :: Platypus :: The reason this badge exists. It hunts with its eyes, ears and nostrils all shut — they close as it dives — so the bill is doing everything, and it carries around forty thousand electroreceptors reading the faint electrical fields of muscle movement in shrimp and larvae. Combined with touch receptors in the same bill, it builds a picture of prey it has never seen.
The Sixth Sense :: Short-beaked Echidna :: The platypus's only living relative in this respect, and the demonstration that the sense came with the lineage rather than with the water. It has electroreceptors too — far fewer, in the tip of the snout — and uses them in wet soil and rotting logs, where the conductivity is good enough for them to work. Two monotremes are the only mammals on earth with this.
The Sixth Sense :: Electric Ray :: The badge member that both senses electricity AND makes it. Paired organs in the disc, built from stacked modified muscle cells, can discharge enough to stun prey and to knock a person over — the same organ type, in the same animal, doing detection and attack. Ancient physicians used living ones as anaesthetic.
The Sixth Sense :: Scalloped Hammerhead :: The head is the instrument. Sharks detect prey with the ampullae of Lorenzini, jelly-filled pores that read tiny voltages, and spreading them across a wide flattened hammer gives a far broader sweep than a pointed snout — which is why this shark can find rays buried in sand it cannot see or smell.
The Sixth Sense :: Great White Shark :: The same ampullae as the hammerhead, and this is the animal in which their sensitivity is most often quoted: fields of a few billionths of a volt per centimetre, which is what a hidden fish's muscles leak. At close range it may be the last sense in use, because a shark's eyes roll back as it bites and it finishes the strike blind.
The Sixth Sense :: West Indian Ocean Coelacanth :: It carries a rostral organ — a large jelly-filled cavity in the snout, present in no living fish outside this lineage — and it is electroreceptive. Coelacanths have been filmed hunting head-down in the dark against rock, which is thought to be the organ in use. A sense from a body plan four hundred million years old.
The Sixth Sense :: Olm :: A blind cave salamander with skin over the eye sockets, and it navigates a lightless flooded cave system partly by electroreception, alongside smell and hearing. It is in this badge as the amphibian: the sense is not a fish trick or a monotreme trick, it turns up wherever an animal has to hunt in water it cannot see through.
The Sixth Sense :: Sea Lamprey :: One of the oldest electroreceptive lineages alive — jawless, from a branch that split before jaws existed — which is why it is here. The electroreceptors of lampreys and those of sharks are thought to descend from the same ancient system, so this animal is evidence about when the sense appeared rather than an example of what it can do.

! --- Seeing With Sound. Echolocation, evolved twice.
Seeing With Sound :: Little Brown Bat :: The workhorse of the badge. It sweeps a frequency downward through each call and reads the returning echo, then speeds the calls up into a buzz as it closes — a hunting bat's last half-second is a stream of two hundred calls, and it can take a mosquito in flight on that information alone. The calls are also loud enough to deafen it, so a muscle in its ear contracts on every shout.
Seeing With Sound :: Greater Horseshoe Bat :: A different method from the little brown bat beside it. It holds a constant frequency instead of sweeping, hangs and listens, and reads the Doppler shift a fluttering insect's wings put into the returning echo — so it detects prey by the beat of the wings rather than by the shape of the body. It even adjusts its own call pitch to keep the echo at the frequency its ear is tuned for.
Seeing With Sound :: Bottlenose Dolphin :: It makes clicks in air passages below the blowhole and focuses them through the fatty melon in its forehead, then receives the echo through the lower jaw, which conducts sound to the ear. It can tell a metal sphere from a plastic one and can read the shape of a fish inside sand — and there is evidence it perceives what another dolphin is inspecting by listening to its echoes.
Seeing With Sound :: Sperm Whale :: The loudest sound made by any animal, at around 230 decibels in water — and it comes from an organ that fills a third of the animal. The spermaceti organ is a sound projector, and the whale uses it to find squid a kilometre down in complete darkness. The clicks are also individually spaced enough that a whale can be identified by them.
Seeing With Sound :: Beluga :: The Arctic case, and the melon is visibly doing the work: a beluga can deform its forehead into different shapes while echolocating, which no other whale demonstrates so obviously. It has to find breathing holes in shifting sea ice from below, in the dark, which is a navigation problem rather than a hunting one.
Seeing With Sound :: Harbor Porpoise :: It uses narrow-band high-frequency clicks well above 100 kHz — too high for a killer whale to hear. That is thought to be the point: the porpoise's main predator cannot detect the sonar it hunts with. It is the badge's example of echolocation shaped by who else is listening.
Seeing With Sound :: Egyptian Fruit Bat :: The exception that shows the trait was invented more than once even among bats. Most fruit bats do not echolocate at all; this one does, and it does it by clicking its TONGUE rather than through the larynx like every other echolocating bat. A separate origin, inside the same order.
Seeing With Sound :: Greater Bulldog Bat :: A bat that fishes. It flies over water reading the tiny ripples a fish makes at the surface — its sonar cannot see through water, so it is detecting the disturbance rather than the animal — then rakes with enlarged hind claws. The badge's point here is what echolocation cannot do: the fish is invisible, only its wake is not.
Seeing With Sound :: Ganges River Dolphin :: Functionally blind, with eyes that have no lens and can do little more than tell light from dark, in a river so silted that vision would be useless anyway. It swims on its side, sweeping one flipper along the bottom, and finds everything by sound. Of all the animals in this badge, it is the one with no alternative.

! --- Heat Vision. Infrared pit organs. Three members, and Ayr's ruling of
! --- 2026-08-29 is that it stands at three.
Heat Vision :: Sidewinder :: A pit viper, so it carries one deep heat-sensing pit between eye and nostril on each side — and the two overlap into something like depth perception in infrared. The signals go to the same part of the brain that handles vision, so a warm mouse in a black desert is not felt, it is SEEN, and can be struck at accurately with the eyes closed.
Heat Vision :: Bushmaster :: The same paired pits as the sidewinder, on the largest viper in the Americas — three metres of ambush predator that may hold one position for weeks waiting. That is what the organ is for here: it does not have to search, it has to detect the moment a warm body passes, in unlit rainforest floor at night.
Heat Vision :: Ball Python :: Not a pit viper, and that is the reason it is in this badge. Pythons evolved heat sensing INDEPENDENTLY, and they did it differently — a row of smaller pits along the lip scales instead of one large pit each side, and different nerve wiring reaching the same result. Two separate inventions of infrared imaging in snakes.

! --- Beyond Violet. Ultraviolet vision.
Beyond Violet :: Common Kestrel :: The classic case, and it is about urine. Vole trails are marked with urine and faeces that reflect ultraviolet, so a kestrel hovering over a field is reading a network of scent-marks as visible lines and can pick the busy runs. It is hunting the map rather than the animal.
Beyond Violet :: Reindeer :: Its eye passes ultraviolet that ours blocks, and in the Arctic that is worth a great deal: snow reflects UV strongly while lichen, fur and urine absorb it, so food, wolves and the marks of other reindeer show as dark against a bright field. Its eye also changes colour between summer and winter, gold to blue, altering how much light it lets through.
Beyond Violet :: Mantis Shrimp :: The most extreme eye in the badge — up to a dozen photoreceptor classes against our three, several of them ultraviolet, using natural filters to split UV into separate channels. The surprise from testing is that it does not discriminate colours more finely than we do; it appears to read them directly, without comparing, which may be faster.
Beyond Violet :: Western Honeybee :: The reason we know flowers are not the colour we see. Bees see ultraviolet, blue and green and are red-blind, and many flowers carry UV patterns — bullseyes and lines converging on the nectar — that are invisible to us and are addressed entirely to the bee. The pattern was co-evolved with a viewer we are not.
Beyond Violet :: Great Tit :: The yellow breast reflects ultraviolet, and the strength of that reflection tracks the bird's condition. Females shown males under UV-filtered light choose differently from females shown the same males under full light — so a display we assumed we were seeing is partly in a channel we cannot see at all.
Beyond Violet :: Budgerigar :: Male and female budgerigars look alike to us and do not to each other: the cheek patches and crown fluoresce under ultraviolet, and blocking that reflection with sunscreen measurably reduces how attractive a bird is judged by the opposite sex. It is a signal that survives right through domestication.
Beyond Violet :: Common Starling :: Its plumage looks black at a distance and is iridescent and UV-reflective close up, and starlings use that in mate choice. It is in the badge as the reminder that this is not a rarity: an ordinary bird on an ordinary roof is exchanging information in a waveband nobody watching it can see.

! --- True North. Magnetoreception.
True North :: Green Sea Turtle :: The strongest evidence for a magnetic MAP rather than just a compass. Hatchlings imprint on the magnetic signature of their natal beach and, decades later and thousands of kilometres away, return to it to nest. Experiments that shift the field around a turtle shift where it thinks it is, which is the difference between knowing north and knowing where you are.
True North :: Arctic Tern :: The longest migration of any animal has to be navigated, and this bird does it partly on the magnetic field — but the badge point is that it uses several systems together: the field, the sun, and the stars, cross-checked. A single cue that failed at the wrong moment would put it in the wrong ocean.
True North :: European Robin :: The species in which magnetoreception was first properly demonstrated, in the 1960s, and it is still where the mechanism is argued out. The leading explanation is chemical and happens in the EYE: a protein called cryptochrome forms light-sensitive radical pairs whose chemistry is affected by the magnetic field. If that is right, the bird may see the field rather than feel it.
True North :: Monarch Butterfly :: It carries a light-dependent inclination compass, so it reads the ANGLE of the field to the earth rather than magnetic polarity — the same solution birds use, in an insect. Combined with a time-compensated sun compass in its antennae, it is enough to take an animal that has never made the journey to a specific Mexican forest.
True North :: Sockeye Salmon :: Records of returning fish across many years match shifts in the earth's magnetic field over the same period, which is why this species is the evidence that a magnetic map is inherited rather than learned — a fish returning to its river has not made the trip before. Smell takes over for the last stretch; the ocean crossing does not have a smell to follow.
True North :: Rock Dove :: The homing pigeon, and the most experimented-on navigator there is. Magnets attached to a bird disrupt its homing; so does releasing it near a magnetic anomaly. It has been part of the argument for decades over whether the sense sits in the beak, the inner ear, or the eye — and the beak answer, long taught, turned out to be iron-rich immune cells rather than nerves.

! --- Through the Glare. Polarised-light vision.
Through the Glare :: Common Cuttlefish :: Colourblind, and still the finest signaller in the sea — it reads polarised light instead, at a sensitivity fine enough to detect small changes in angle. It can also SEND on that channel: the arms carry polarisation patterns it can switch, so two cuttlefish can hold a conversation invisible to a fish that lacks the receptor.
Through the Glare :: Mantis Shrimp :: The only animals known to see CIRCULARLY polarised light — a twist in the wave that no other eye has been shown to detect — and some species reflect it from their own bodies. That makes it a completely private channel: a signal that can only be seen by the species sending it.
Through the Glare :: Giant Pacific Octopus :: Also colourblind, and also polarisation-sensitive, which is the standing explanation for the puzzle of how an animal that cannot see colour matches its background so precisely. It is here beside the cuttlefish because the two share the ability and use it differently — one signals with it, one hides with it.
Through the Glare :: Mimic Octopus :: The same sensitivity, put to the badge's most demanding job. Its impressions of a sole, a lionfish and a sea snake are judged by the eyes of the animals it is fooling, and polarisation vision gives it information about surface and texture that a colour-blind eye would otherwise lack.
Through the Glare :: Western Honeybee :: How a bee knows where the sun is on a cloudy day. Scattered skylight is polarised in a pattern centred on the sun, and a band of specialised receptors at the top of the bee's eye reads it — so a patch of blue is enough to fix the sun's position, and the waggle dance, which is given in angles relative to the sun, still works.
Through the Glare :: Monarch Butterfly :: The same skylight compass as the honeybee, doing a much longer job: holding a heading across a continent. Its sun compass is time-compensated by a clock in the antennae, and polarised light lets that compass keep working when the sun itself is behind cloud — which over several thousand kilometres it often is.

! --- Lights On. Bioluminescence - light the animal MAKES. The Deathstalker is
! --- not here and must not be: fluorescence is a different thing entirely.
Lights On :: Humpback Anglerfish :: The lure is not hers. The bulb on the end of the rod is a chamber farming symbiotic bacteria, and the light is theirs — the fish supplies housing and nutrients and controls the display by shuttering it with tissue. The bacteria involved have reduced genomes typical of organisms that can no longer live independently, so neither partner is complete alone.
Lights On :: Black Dragonfish :: Light organs in rows along the flanks, plus a lit barbel hanging from the chin, in an animal about forty centimetres long. It is the pattern that matters: a line of lights along the belly is counter-illumination, erasing the silhouette from below, while a light on a stalk in front of the mouth is a lure. The same animal is hiding and advertising at once.
Lights On :: Alarm Jellyfish :: The burglar alarm. Seized, it fires a rotating wheel of blue light that can be seen a long way off — not to startle the attacker but to advertise it, and draw something bigger down onto it. A lure copied from this display is what finally filmed a giant squid alive, after every attempt with bright lights had failed.
Lights On :: Common Eastern Firefly :: The land case, and the one with a predator in it. Each species has its own flash pattern, and females of a DIFFERENT firefly genus have learned to mimic this species' answering flash, luring its males in and eating them. The signal is honest until something starts forging it.
Lights On :: Vampire Squid :: It has no ink sac, because ink is useless where there is no light to hide in. Instead it ejects a cloud of glowing mucus full of light-producing particles and leaves in the dark behind it, and it carries large light organs at the fin tips that it can flash while it turns itself inside out into a spined ball.
Lights On :: Glacier Lanternfish :: The most ordinary bioluminescence in the ocean, which is exactly why it is here. Lanternfish carry species-specific patterns of small light organs along the underside — counter-illumination and identification at once — and there are so many lanternfish that this is probably the commonest way light gets made on earth.
Lights On :: Viperfish :: Photophores along the belly for counter-illumination, and a lure on the drawn-out first ray of the dorsal fin held out over the head. It is the demonstration that the lure has to be positioned as well as lit: the light is dangled in front of a mouth that cannot close over its own teeth.
Lights On :: Cookiecutter Shark :: The strangest use in the badge. Its underside glows, except for a dark band under the throat — so from below it reads as a small fish silhouetted against dim light, and whatever comes up to eat that small fish is what gets a plug of flesh taken out of it. The light is not camouflage and it is not a lure exactly. It is bait shaped like prey.
Lights On :: Silver Hatchetfish :: Counter-illumination done precisely. The light organs on its underside are tuned to match the colour and brightness of the daylight filtering down, and it can adjust the output as the light above changes, so a predator looking up sees no shadow where the fish is. Its eyes point permanently upward, watching the light it is matching.
Lights On :: Stoplight Loosejaw :: It makes RED light, which almost nothing in the deep sea can see — an invisible searchlight. And to see the red it produces, it uses a pigment derived from chlorophyll, taken second-hand from copepods that ate algae. An animal borrowing plant chemistry to build a private waveband.
Lights On :: Bloody-belly Comb Jelly :: The reason a transparent animal needs a red stomach: a swallowed meal that glows would light the eater up from inside. The comb jelly's own glow is separate, and the rainbow running along its combs is not light it makes at all — that is a submersible's lamp being split by beating hairs, which is worth saying because it is the commonest mistake made about this animal.
Lights On :: Helmet Jellyfish :: A deep red-brown bell that avoids light so completely it will not cross a lit layer of water — and it luminesces itself when disturbed. It is in the badge for the contrast: an animal that makes light and flees from it, which is the ordinary condition down there rather than a contradiction.

! --- Grow It Back. Regeneration, and the members are not all doing the same job.
Grow It Back :: Axolotl :: The benchmark. It regrows a whole limb — bone, muscle, nerve, blood vessels, skin, in the right order and the right size — and can do it repeatedly without scarring, along with jaws, spinal cord, parts of the heart and parts of the brain. Cells at the stump reverse their own specialisation into a mass called a blastema and start again. Mammals form scar tissue at exactly the point where this animal forms a blastema.
Grow It Back :: Common Brittle Star :: Regeneration used as an escape. The arms break at pre-formed fracture planes, so shedding one is a controlled act rather than an injury, and the animal leaves the arm behind still writhing while it goes. The arm regrows. In some brittle stars a fragment of the central disc can rebuild the entire animal.
Grow It Back :: Tiger Tail Sea Cucumber :: It throws away its internal organs. Attacked, it expels sticky white threads and part of its own gut through the anus to entangle whatever is holding it, then walks off and regrows the lot over weeks. It is the badge's clearest case of regeneration as a weapon: the organs are ammunition because they can be replaced.
Grow It Back :: Sunflower Sea Star :: Up to twenty-four arms and it regrows any of them, which is ordinary for a sea star — what is not ordinary is that the ability did not save it. Sea star wasting disease dissolved its populations from Alaska to Mexico from 2013, and it is now Critically Endangered. An animal that can rebuild an arm cannot rebuild against a disease that liquefies the whole body.
Grow It Back :: Leopard Gecko :: The honest limit of the badge. It drops its tail at a fracture plane to escape, and the replacement is not a copy — the vertebrae do not come back, and what grows is a rod of cartilage covered in different scales, shorter and blunter. Regeneration here is a repair that leaves the animal visibly different, which is the usual vertebrate answer.
Grow It Back :: Great Crested Newt :: The adult land-living version of what the axolotl does in water, and it goes further in one respect: it regrows the LENS of its eye, from the iris, and can do it over and over into old age. A newt that has been made to regrow a lens eighteen times produced a normal lens the eighteenth time.
Grow It Back :: Star Ascidian :: Whole-body regeneration from circulating cells. This is a colonial sea squirt, and a fragment of its blood-vessel network with no zooids left in it at all can rebuild an entire functioning colony — the animal reassembles from cells in the blood. Nothing else in this badge starts from that little.
Grow It Back :: Sea Lamprey :: A vertebrate that repairs its own spinal cord. Cut it through completely and the lamprey is paralysed, and then over ten to twelve weeks the nerve fibres regrow across the gap and it swims normally again. In a mammal that injury is permanent, which is precisely why this animal sits in neuroscience laboratories.
Grow It Back :: Ochre Sea Star :: It regrows arms like the sunflower star beside it, and it is here for what that means ecologically rather than anatomically. This is the animal keystone predation was defined on: remove it and the mussels take the whole shore. An animal that can lose arms to waves and gulls and carry on is one that stays in place long enough to hold a system open.

! --- See-Through. Transparency, and each one is transparent by a different trick.
See-Through :: Fleischmann's Glass Frog :: You can watch the heart beat through the skin of the belly. And when it sleeps it becomes MORE transparent: it packs almost ninety percent of its red blood cells into the liver, so the blood stops colouring it — a concentration of cells that would clot fatally in any other vertebrate. It reverses it on waking.
See-Through :: Glasswing Butterfly :: The wing membrane is clear because it lacks the coloured scales of an ordinary butterfly wing, and it does not glint because the surface is covered in tiny irregular pillars that stop light reflecting off it. Transparency is not enough on its own — an unreflective surface is what makes it invisible rather than shiny.
See-Through :: Googly-eyed Glass Squid :: Transparent everywhere except the two things that cannot be: the eyes, which must absorb light to work, and the gut, which is full of what it ate. It holds both vertically so they throw the narrowest possible shadow downward — the residual opacity is managed by posture.
See-Through :: Macropinna Barreleye :: The transparency is a window rather than camouflage. Its whole head is capped in a clear fluid-filled dome, and the tubular eyes sit inside it looking up through the animal's own skull. Nobody knew the dome existed for sixty-five years because it collapses in a net, so the fish was described from specimens that had lost the feature.
See-Through :: Venus's Girdle :: A metre and a half of transparent ribbon, and the colour running along it is not pigment. Rows of beating hairs split light into a moving band of colour as it swims, so the animal is invisible and iridescent at the same time — an effect produced by movement rather than by anything in the body.
See-Through :: Moon Jelly :: Around ninety-five percent water, and the only part with any substance is the ring of four horseshoe-shaped gonads at the top, which is why the badge's simplest transparency is also its most recognisable silhouette. It is here as the common case: most transparent animals in the sea are gelatinous, because water is the cheapest thing to be made of if you want light to pass through.
See-Through :: Deepstaria :: A transparent sheet up to a metre across with almost no tentacles, which billows and closes around whatever drifts inside. Films of it usually show a bright isopod riding within the bell — a passenger visible only because the animal around it is not.

! --- Sub-Zero. Antifreeze proteins, and one member does something else entirely.
Sub-Zero :: Antarctic Icefish :: The Southern Ocean fish that antifreeze glycoproteins were discovered in, and it carries a second adaptation on top: no haemoglobin at all, so its blood is colourless and carries oxygen dissolved directly in the plasma. Both only work in water that is near freezing and rich in oxygen. It has solved the cold twice over and can survive nowhere else.
Sub-Zero :: Antarctic Toothfish :: It lives in water below the freezing point of its own blood. Antifreeze glycoproteins bind to any ice crystal that starts to form and stop it growing — the crystal is still there and simply cannot get larger, which is a different mechanism from lowering the freezing point. The proteins do not melt ice; they arrest it.
Sub-Zero :: Arctic Cod :: The same chemistry at the other pole, in an animal that lives INSIDE the ice — in the brine channels threading through a floe, where the water stays liquid because it is too salty to freeze. Antifreeze proteins here are not protection against an unusual winter; they are the condition of the habitat.
Sub-Zero :: Arctic Char :: The freshwater member, and the problem is different in fresh water: it freezes at a higher temperature and there is no salt to depress it, so a fish under lake ice faces cold rather than sub-zero liquid. Char live further north than any other freshwater fish and overwinter under metres of ice, which is the cold tolerance this badge is about even where the chemistry differs from the marine members beside it.
Sub-Zero :: Wood Frog :: The one that does not resist freezing at all — it allows it. Up to about two-thirds of the water in its body turns to ice, the heart stops and breathing ceases for weeks. Its liver floods the tissues with glucose beforehand, which keeps the cells themselves from freezing while the water around them does. Everything else in this badge stays liquid. This one does not, and thaws anyway.

! --- Second Bite. Pharyngeal jaws.
Second Bite :: Giant Moray :: The one that made the news, in 2007. Its second set of jaws does not sit passively in the throat — it launches FORWARD into the mouth, grips prey and drags it back down. A moray cannot create suction the way most fish do because of its narrow head, so it evolved a mechanical way to swallow instead. No other vertebrate is known to do this.
Second Bite :: Stoplight Parrotfish :: The same second jaws used as a mill rather than a grip. Behind the beak that scrapes coral, the pharyngeal teeth grind rock and algae into powder — and what comes out the other end is sand. A large parrotfish makes hundreds of kilos of white beach a year, so the second bite in this species is a geological process.
Second Bite :: Bluestreak Cleaner Wrasse :: The wrasse arrangement: pharyngeal jaws that crush. It is why a small fish can live on parasites with hard shells and skin scraped from a predator's mouth — the food is dealt with in the throat rather than at the front. It is also being done inside the mouth of a fish that could eat it, which is the other half of this animal's story.
Second Bite :: Discus :: The cichlid, and the reason this badge matters beyond curiosity. Pharyngeal jaws freed the mouth from having to process food, so the mouth could specialise for catching it — and cichlids then radiated into hundreds of species in the African lakes with different mouths and the same throat machinery. This is a textbook key innovation: one change that opens a family's whole future.

! --- Built Wrong on Purpose. Selective breeding and what it costs. These cards say
! --- what the STANDARD asks for and what that does; the animal's own entry says
! --- the rest.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: French Bulldog :: The standard asks for a flat face on a wide, heavy body. That combination produces both problems: the airway is compressed into a shortened skull, and the puppy's head is too broad for the mother's pelvis. Most litters are delivered by caesarean, so the breed's continued existence depends on veterinary surgery. It became the most registered dog in several countries while that was true.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: Bulldog :: The oldest case in the badge and the most studied. Its shape was made in the century after bull-baiting was banned, purely for the show ring, and the changes have been measured: skull, spine and airway all altered enough that a large majority cannot mate or give birth unaided. A 2016 genetic study found the breed may lack the diversity left to correct itself from within.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: Pug :: Two separate costs from two separate features. The flat face brings the same airway problem as the bulldogs beside it, and the tight corkscrew tail is caused by malformed vertebrae — the same malformation, further up the spine, causes paralysis. The tail is not decoration attached to a healthy animal; it is a visible sign of a skeletal condition.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: Persian :: The extreme flat face in cats. Selecting the face inward moved the tear ducts out of alignment, so the eyes water constantly and stain, and the shortened jaw crowds the teeth. It also carries inherited polycystic kidney disease at high frequency — a gene that can now be tested for, which makes it the clearest case in the badge of a cost that is optional.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: Scottish Fold :: The cruellest entry here, because the trait and the disease are the same thing. The folded ear is caused by a cartilage disorder, and it does not stop at the ear: EVERY cat with folded ears has abnormal cartilage in its joints, and many develop painful arthritis young. There is no healthy version of the look. Several countries have moved to ban breeding it.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: Manx :: The taillessness is dominant and lethal in double dose, so the breed cannot be bred true and must be crossed with tailed cats forever. Even a single copy shortens the spine in ways that reach the nerves at the base of it, and the associated bladder and bowel problems have their own name. A breed defined by a gene it can never carry twice.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: Munchkin :: The same lethal-in-double-dose arithmetic as the Manx beside it, applied to leg length. What separates it in this badge is timing: it was recognised as a breed in the 1990s, long after the costs of this kind of selection were understood, and one judge resigned over the decision. It is the case where nobody could claim not to have known.
Built Wrong on Purpose :: Bull Terrier :: The one where the change is photographed. The breed's early dogs had ordinary terrier skulls with a clear stop between muzzle and forehead; a century of selecting for a downward curve produced the unbroken egg-shaped profile of today. The badge usually has to argue from anatomy. Here the before and after are both in the record.

! --- No Bones About It. Invertebrate giants - and the card says what normally
! --- limits that body plan, and how this one got around it.
No Bones About It :: Giant Squid :: The eye is the record: about twenty-seven centimetres, the largest of any animal, on a body held up by water rather than by bone. It has no skeleton at all — a squid is a muscular hydrostat, which is why it can be enormous and still collapse into a bucket. Almost every specimen has been dead or dying at the surface, and it was not filmed alive in its own habitat until 2012.
No Bones About It :: Colossal Squid :: Heavier than the giant squid and shorter, which is the distinction people usually get backwards. Its arms carry swivelling hooks rather than only suckers, and it lives in Southern Ocean water cold enough that its metabolism runs very slowly — cold and no skeleton together are how an invertebrate reaches half a tonne.
No Bones About It :: Japanese Spider Crab :: The largest leg span of any arthropod, up to about three and a half metres. Arthropods are limited by having to shed the whole external skeleton to grow, and by how much weight that skeleton can carry in air — this one gets around both by living deep in cold water, where its own weight is largely supported and it can afford to grow slowly for decades.
No Bones About It :: Coconut Crab :: The largest land arthropod there is, which is the harder record: on land the exoskeleton has to hold the animal up unaided. It manages about four kilograms, climbs trees, and has the strongest pinch measured in any crustacean. It is also the limit — nothing bigger walks on land, and this is close to why.
No Bones About It :: Lion's Mane Jelly :: The longest tentacles of any jellyfish, trailing tens of metres behind a bell that can exceed two metres. Almost none of it is tissue: the animal is water held in shape by a thin structural jelly, which is the cheapest possible way for an invertebrate to occupy an enormous volume.
No Bones About It :: Giant Siphonophore :: The one that is arguably not an individual at all. It grows from a single fertilised egg into a chain of specialised bodies — swimmers, stingers, digesters, breeders — none of which can live alone, and the whole reaches tens of metres. It is in this badge to complicate it: the largest invertebrates raise the question of what one animal even is.
No Bones About It :: Giant Isopod :: A woodlouse relative at half a metre, and the standing explanation is deep-sea gigantism — cold water, high oxygen and scarce food together favour animals that grow large and slowly and can then wait. One in captivity refused every meal for over five years and appeared perfectly healthy.
No Bones About It :: Giant Gippsland Earthworm :: Up to three metres of animal in Australian clay, and it is the badge's fragile giant: it cannot be handled without injury, it lays one egg capsule a year, and it lives entirely within a few river catchments in South Gippsland. A giant with no armour, no speed and nowhere else to go.
No Bones About It :: Goliath Beetle :: Among the heaviest insects alive, up to about a hundred grams as a larva. Insect size is limited by breathing: they have no lungs, and take oxygen through tubes that carry it directly to the tissue, which stops working well beyond a certain body thickness. That ceiling is why the giant insects of the Carboniferous needed an atmosphere richer in oxygen than today's.
No Bones About It :: Chan's Megastick :: The longest insect ever recorded — over half a metre of body, and more than sixty centimetres with the legs stretched out. It gets around the breathing limit the goliath beetle runs into by being long rather than thick: a stick insect is never far from its own surface, so the oxygen tubes never have far to reach.
No Bones About It :: Antarctic Sea Spider :: Polar gigantism, and an animal with almost no body to speak of — the gut and the reproductive organs run out into the legs because there is no trunk to hold them. It has no lungs and no gills either and takes oxygen straight through its cuticle, which is exactly why cold, oxygen-rich Antarctic water lets it reach the span of a dinner plate.
No Bones About It :: Giant Clam :: The largest bivalve there has been, past a metre and two hundred kilograms, and it is here because it solves the size problem from the other direction. It does not chase food: it farms algae in its own exposed mantle, so most of its energy arrives free with the sunlight and it can afford to put everything into shell and tissue.

! --- Same Answer Twice. Convergence. Every card names its partner, because two
! --- gliding mammals in a list demonstrates nothing on its own.
Same Answer Twice :: Flying Squirrel :: Paired with the SUGAR GLIDER in this badge. It is a placental mammal and a rodent; the sugar glider is a marsupial and no relation. Their lineages separated well over a hundred million years ago and neither inherited gliding from the other — both grew the same membrane between wrist and ankle, and both steer with a flattened tail, because the problem was the same one.
Same Answer Twice :: Thylacine :: Paired with the GREY WOLF in this badge, and it is the most quoted convergence there is. A marsupial arrived at the skull, the teeth and the running body of a placental dog so exactly that its bones are used to test whether students can tell the two apart. The differences are in the pouch, the gape and the jaw muscles — everything the shape did not need to change.
Same Answer Twice :: Grey Wolf :: Paired with the THYLACINE in this badge. It is the placental original of a shape a marsupial reinvented on an island continent, and the pairing is what makes it a claim rather than a resemblance: two lineages separated by about 160 million years, given the same job — chase down prey in open country — returning the same animal.
Same Answer Twice :: Smilodon :: Paired with GORGONOPS in this badge. Both grew sabre teeth and both are synapsids, but they are separated by roughly 250 million years and by the entire origin of mammals in between — the gorgonopsian died in the Permian extinction, long before the first cat. Sabre teeth have appeared independently in at least five separate lineages, which is the point.
Same Answer Twice :: Gorgonops :: Paired with SMILODON in this badge, and the older half of the pair by a quarter of a billion years. It was the dominant land predator of the late Permian, with sabre teeth and legs already tucked under the body rather than sprawled at the sides. Everything about it looks like a preview of the mammal predators that would not exist for another hundred million years.
Same Answer Twice :: Shonisaurus :: Paired with the BOTTLENOSE DOLPHIN in this badge. It was a reptile that went back to the sea in the Triassic and arrived at the dolphin's body — streamlined, flippers in front, vertical fluke behind — around two hundred million years before any dolphin existed. Both started as four-legged land animals and both got the same answer.
Same Answer Twice :: Bottlenose Dolphin :: Paired with SHONISAURUS in this badge. A mammal whose ancestors walked on land, returning to water and converging on the same shape an ichthyosaur reached in the Triassic. The one visible tell is the tail: a dolphin's fluke beats up and down, because that is how a running mammal's spine bends, and an ichthyosaur's beat side to side, because that is how a reptile's does.
Same Answer Twice :: Giant Anteater :: One of three unrelated ant-eaters in this badge, with the SUNDA PANGOLIN and the AARDVARK. Placental, from South America, in the order Pilosa with the sloths. No teeth at all, a sixty-centimetre tongue that flicks a hundred and fifty times a minute, and forelimbs built to open a nest — the same toolkit, arrived at separately.
Same Answer Twice :: Sunda Pangolin :: One of three unrelated ant-eaters in this badge, with the GIANT ANTEATER and the AARDVARK. Its closest living relatives are the carnivorans — cats, dogs and bears — not the other two. It reached the same long snout, the same toothless mouth and the same digging claws from a completely different branch, and added keratin armour nothing else in the group has.
Same Answer Twice :: Aardvark :: One of three unrelated ant-eaters in this badge, with the GIANT ANTEATER and the SUNDA PANGOLIN, and the most distant of the three: it belongs to the African afrotheres, whose other members are elephants, manatees and hyraxes. Three lineages that separated before any of them ate an ant have converged on one body because termites are the same problem everywhere.

! --- The Island Rule. Islands push both ways, so every card says which way.
The Island Rule :: Komodo Dragon :: GIGANTISM. The largest lizard alive, on a handful of Indonesian islands with no large mammal predator or competitor to keep it down — so a monitor took the role a big carnivorous mammal would hold on the mainland. Its ancestors were already large in Australia, which is the honest caveat: the island did not invent the size so much as preserve and permit it.
The Island Rule :: Coconut Crab :: GIGANTISM, and on land, which makes it the badge's hardest case. It is the largest land arthropod there is, and it lives on remote Indo-Pacific islands where there is nothing much to eat it once it outgrows being a hermit crab. Where rats and people arrive, the large individuals go first.
The Island Rule :: Pinta Island Tortoise :: GIGANTISM, and the whole Galápagos pattern in one animal: reptiles reaching enormous size on islands with no mammal grazers or predators, and then differing island by island in shell shape according to what there was to reach. This one ended with Lonesome George in 2012, which is the other half of the rule — island giants have nowhere to retreat to.
The Island Rule :: Aldabra Giant Tortoise :: GIGANTISM, and the survivor. It is one of only two giant tortoise lineages left on earth, on one atoll, and it does not merely live in its habitat but maintains it — grazing keeps a low turf of plants that exists because the tortoises are there. Island gigantism producing an animal that shapes the island.
The Island Rule :: Elephant Bird :: GIGANTISM, and the record: the heaviest bird known, at around half a tonne, on Madagascar. Its egg held roughly the volume of about a hundred and fifty chicken eggs and is the largest single cell ever produced by any animal. Gone within the last thousand years, after people arrived.
The Island Rule :: Haast's Eagle :: GIGANTISM, in the predator slot. It is the largest eagle known, on islands where the only large prey was moa and there were no mammal carnivores to compete with — so a bird took the role a big cat would hold elsewhere. Its genetics show it grew to that size fast, from a much smaller ancestor, which is island gigantism caught in the act.
The Island Rule :: Bali Tiger :: DWARFISM, and the only member of this badge running that direction. It was the smallest tiger there has been, on a small island with small prey and no room for a large-bodied predator — the mirror image of what the island did to the reptiles and birds beside it. It was extinct by about the 1940s, which is the rule's other cost: a dwarfed population is a small one.

! --- Grounded. Flightlessness where nothing hunted on the ground - and most of
! --- these are gone, which is the hard half.
Grounded :: Kākāpō :: The only flightless parrot, and the heaviest — nocturnal, and it booms from a bowl scraped in the ground to carry the call kilometres. Its defence against a hunting bird was to freeze, which works against an eye and does nothing against a cat's nose. Down to fifty-one animals in 1995; every living bird is named and known individually.
Grounded :: Kiwi :: Flightless, nocturnal, with nostrils at the TIP of the bill — the only bird with them there — and a sense of smell it hunts by, in a country that had no land mammals to smell it back. Its egg is up to a fifth of the female's body weight, one of the largest relative to the mother of any bird.
Grounded :: Cassowary :: The one that did not need to hide. It is a heavy flightless bird with a bony casque and a dagger-like inner claw, and it can be genuinely dangerous — flightlessness on an island where the answer was to be too big and too armed rather than too quiet. It is also the main disperser of large rainforest fruit seeds nothing else can swallow.
Grounded :: Emu :: The mainland exception in a badge mostly about islands: it is flightless in continental Australia, where dingoes and eagles exist. It got away with it by being fast — it runs at around fifty kilometres an hour and covers enormous distances following rain — which is a different answer from every island bird here.
Grounded :: Ostrich :: The largest bird alive and the fastest thing on two legs, on a continent full of large predators. Like the emu it traded flight for running rather than for safety, and its two-toed foot is unique among birds: a hoof-like arrangement built entirely for speed over open ground.
Grounded :: Dodo :: The badge's byword, and almost everything popularly believed about it is wrong. It was a large ground pigeon, not fat and stupid but adapted to an island with no mammal predators, and it was probably eaten less by sailors than by the rats, pigs and monkeys they landed with. It was gone within about a century of first contact, and there is no complete specimen anywhere.
Grounded :: Giant Moa :: Nine species of flightless bird in New Zealand, the largest standing over three metres, and they are the only birds known to have lost their wings ENTIRELY — no wing bones at all, not even vestigial ones. Hunted out within roughly a century or two of Polynesian settlement, taking Haast's eagle with them.
Grounded :: Elephant Bird :: The heaviest bird known, flightless on Madagascar, and the badge member that also holds a place in The Island Rule beside it. Genetics settled a long argument about its relatives with an unlikely answer: its closest living relative is the kiwi, a small nocturnal bird on the other side of the Indian Ocean.
Grounded :: Guam Rail :: The one that came back. Flightless on Guam, and eaten to extinction in the wild by the introduced brown tree snake by 1987 — then bred in captivity and released onto snake-free islands, and downlisted from Extinct in the Wild in 2019. It is only the second bird ever to recover from that listing.
Grounded :: Great Auk :: The northern hemisphere's flightless seabird, and the closest thing the Atlantic had to a penguin — to which it is unrelated. It bred on a handful of remote islands and was killed for feathers, meat and oil. The last confirmed pair was taken on Eldey in 1844, along with the egg they were incubating, for a collector.

! --- Unchanged. The badge is about how long the SHAPE has lasted, not about an
! --- animal that stopped evolving. Every card has to keep that straight.
Unchanged :: West Indian Ocean Coelacanth :: The famous one, and the phrase "living fossil" does it a disservice. The lineage is over four hundred million years old and the body plan has held, but this species is not the fossil one — it is a modern animal with a modern genome, and it gives birth to live young after around three years, the longest gestation known in any fish.
Unchanged :: Chambered Nautilus :: The last of the externally shelled cephalopods, a group that once filled the seas — and it still works the shell the old way, adjusting gas in the sealed chambers to hover. Its eye has no lens at all: a pinhole, open to the sea, which is what a cephalopod eye looked like before the squid and octopus built better ones.
Unchanged :: Tuatara :: Not a lizard. It is the only survivor of Rhynchocephalia, an order that split from lizards and snakes around 250 million years ago, so its nearest relatives have been dead since the age of dinosaurs. It has two rows of upper teeth that the lower row slots between, and it keeps growing and stays active at temperatures that would immobilise a lizard.
Unchanged :: Horseshoe Crab :: Not a crab — closer to spiders and scorpions — and the shape is recognisable in fossils around 450 million years old. It is also the badge's most useful animal: its blue, copper-based blood clots on contact with bacterial toxin, which is why it is bled in the hundreds of thousands every year to test vaccines and implants for contamination.
Unchanged :: Frilled Shark :: A shark with the jaw at the front of the head rather than underneath, six gill slits, and three hundred backward-pointing teeth in twenty-five rows — an arrangement more like the sharks of the Devonian than like anything swimming beside it today. It is thought to lunge and swallow whole, and to carry its young for a very long time.
Unchanged :: Southern Velvet Worm :: An onychophoran, from a group whose body plan appears in the Cambrian — soft-bodied, with stubby unjointed legs, sitting between the annelid worms and the arthropods. It hunts by squirting two jets of glue that set in seconds. Half a billion years of that shape, and it is still doing something no arthropod does.
Unchanged :: Pacific Hagfish :: Jawless, from a lineage that split before jaws existed, with a skull made of cartilage and no vertebrae at all — the badge's deepest cut into the vertebrate family tree. It defends itself by turning a bucket of seawater into slime in seconds, and then ties itself in a knot to scrape the slime off.
Unchanged :: Japanese Sea Lily :: A stalked crinoid, from the group that carpeted Palaeozoic seafloors and is now mostly too deep to notice. It looks like a plant and it is an animal, and the stalk is not a permanent commitment: it can detach, crawl away on its arms and re-anchor, which was only confirmed by filming one doing it.
Unchanged :: Bluntnose Sixgill Shark :: It kept a gill slit that almost every other shark gave up. Six instead of five is the older arrangement, shared with sharks from the Jurassic, and it belongs to a small handful of surviving lineages beside the frilled shark in this badge. It grows to five metres and most people have never heard of it.

! --- End of the Line. The last surviving member of an entire branch - and the
! --- card names the branch, because "rare" is not the same claim.
End of the Line :: Aardvark :: The only living species in its ORDER, Tubulidentata — the deepest solitude in this badge. Everything else here is the last of a family or a genus; this is the last of a branch that has been separate since near the base of the placental mammals. Its teeth are unlike any other mammal's: no enamel, no roots, just bundles of dentine tubes that wear away and keep growing.
End of the Line :: Tuatara :: The only survivor of Rhynchocephalia, an order that split from the lizards and snakes about 250 million years ago and was diverse through the age of dinosaurs. One species remains, on offshore New Zealand islands. It also keeps a functional third eye on the top of the head in juveniles, complete with lens and retina.
End of the Line :: Platypus :: The only living species in its family, and with the four echidnas the only egg-laying mammals left. It is the last of a lineage that separated from all other mammals around 170 million years ago — and its genome shows it, carrying bird-like and reptile-like sex chromosomes and duplicated milk genes in one animal.
End of the Line :: Aye-aye :: The only living species in its family, Daubentoniidae, and a lemur that has become something no other primate is. It taps a branch, listens for the hollow with enormous ears, gnaws in with rodent-like ever-growing incisors, and fishes the grub out with a middle finger that is thin, elongated and mounted on a ball-and-socket joint.
End of the Line :: Okapi :: The honest entry in this badge, and it makes the concept precise. It is not alone: it is one of TWO living giraffids, the other being the giraffe. A family reduced to two species with nothing between them is what most of these branches look like just before the end, and the okapi was unknown to science until 1901.
End of the Line :: Maned Wolf :: The only species in Chrysocyon, and not a wolf or a fox — the last of a South American canid radiation that was much larger before the Americas joined and northern carnivores came south. It looks like a fox on stilts because the legs are for seeing over tall grass, and about half its diet is a fruit.
End of the Line :: Numbat :: The only living species in its family, Myrmecobiidae, and the only marsupial that is active entirely by day — because termites are, and it eats up to twenty thousand of them daily. It has more teeth than any other land mammal, around fifty, and barely uses them.
End of the Line :: Hoatzin :: One species in its own ORDER, and its position on the bird family tree is still argued over because there is nothing to compare it with. Its chicks are born with functional CLAWS on their wings and climb back up into the nest tree after dropping into the water to escape — and the adult ferments leaves in an enlarged crop, which is a cow's solution in a bird.

! --- Made By Us. Domestication, and what it does to a body.
Made By Us :: Domestic Fox :: The experiment that showed the rest of this badge is one process. Belyayev's team in Siberia selected silver foxes for one thing only — whether a cub would approach a hand — and within a few dozen generations got floppy ears, curled tails, piebald coats, shorter muzzles and a longer breeding season. Nothing about appearance was ever chosen. Tameness dragged the rest along behind it.
Made By Us :: Silkworm :: The most completely domesticated animal there is. Five thousand years of selection have left a moth that cannot fly, cannot find a mate unaided, has lost its fear of predators and does not exist in the wild anywhere at all. Its ancestor is a different species. This is the far end of the badge: an animal that has no independent existence to return to.
Made By Us :: Chicken :: The most numerous bird on earth by an enormous margin, and the modern broiler is the sharpest illustration in the badge — it reaches slaughter weight in about six weeks, roughly a quarter of the time it took in 1950, on less feed. Its body is being changed faster than at any point in its history, and archaeologists have argued the bones will mark this period in the rock record.
Made By Us :: Fancy Pigeon :: Hundreds of breeds so different that a naturalist shown only the birds would file them in separate genera, and every one is the same species as the pigeon on the pavement. Darwin kept them, bred them, and put them in the FIRST chapter of On the Origin of Species — before making any argument at all — because pigeon fanciers were living proof that selection reshapes an animal.
Made By Us :: Goldfish :: A drab olive carp from East Asia, bred over a thousand years into fish with no dorsal fin, upward-facing eyes, fluid-filled sacs under the eyes, and tails that make swimming difficult. It is the badge's aquatic case and the same story as the dog breeds: features selected for how they look from outside the animal.
Made By Us :: English Budgie :: The show line of a wild Australian parrot, bred to nearly twice the weight with a domed head and face feathering that can cover the eyes. It is calmer and slower than a pet-type budgie, which owners often prefer, and it lives noticeably less long — usually well under ten years against fifteen or more for the wild type.
Made By Us :: Angora Rabbit :: Bred out of the ability to shed. Its coat never stops growing, so it must be harvested every few months or it mats to the skin — and a rabbit grooms by licking and cannot bring up a hairball, so the wool it swallows can block its gut fatally. An animal that cannot survive without a person holding a comb.

! --- Back From the Dead. Lazarus taxa - written off, then found alive.
Back From the Dead :: West Indian Ocean Coelacanth :: The founding case. The lineage was believed to have ended with the non-avian dinosaurs until a museum curator, Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, found one on a trawler's catch at East London in 1938 and could not identify it. Sixty-five million years of assumed absence, ended by somebody looking properly at a fish on a quay.
Back From the Dead :: Chacoan Peccary :: Described from FOSSILS first, in 1930, and assumed long extinct — then found alive in the Gran Chaco in 1975. The local people had always known it. That is the shape of most of this badge: the animal was not lost, only unrecorded, and "rediscovery" means science catching up with people who lived there.
Back From the Dead :: Woolly Flying Squirrel :: Known from a handful of skins collected in the nineteenth century and not seen again for most of the twentieth, so it was presumed gone — until it was found alive in northern Pakistan in 1994. Local collectors had been harvesting deposits from its cave floors as a traditional medicine the whole time.
Back From the Dead :: Silver-backed Chevrotain :: Described in 1910 and then unrecorded for nearly thirty years after the last known specimen, until camera traps in Vietnamese coastal scrub photographed it in 2019 — found by asking local people where to put the cameras. It is the badge's newest entry and the reminder that the list is not closed.

! --- Not What It Looks Like. Misleading names.
Not What It Looks Like :: Red Panda :: Neither red panda nor giant panda is a bear-and-raccoon question with one answer: the giant panda is a bear, and this animal is the only living member of its OWN family, Ailuridae. It was named panda first, and the giant panda took the name from it. It eats bamboo, and it grew a false thumb to hold it — independently of the giant panda, which grew one too.
Not What It Looks Like :: Raccoon Dog :: A canid — a genuine dog, related to foxes — that looks like a raccoon and is not remotely one. It is also the only dog that hibernates, and the only one that regularly climbs trees. Its fur is sold under the name "Asiatic raccoon", which is where the name does actual harm.
Not What It Looks Like :: Aardwolf :: A hyena that gave up. It is in the hyena family and it does not hunt or scavenge: it licks up termites with a sticky tongue, up to a quarter of a million a night, and its teeth have dwindled to pegs. The name means "earth wolf" and it is neither a wolf nor doing anything a hyena is famous for.
Not What It Looks Like :: Maned Wolf :: Not a wolf, not a fox, and not closely related to either — the only species in its own genus. The long legs are not for speed but for seeing over tall grass, and roughly half its diet is a tomato-like fruit. It is in this badge and in End of the Line for the same underlying reason: there is nothing else like it to name it after.
Not What It Looks Like :: Mountain Goat :: Not a goat. Its nearest relatives are the chamois and the gorals, and the resemblance to a goat is a shared answer to standing on rock rather than shared ancestry. The hooves make the point: a hard sharp rim for cutting into ledges with a soft rubbery pad inside it, which is an edge and a sole in the same foot.
Not What It Looks Like :: Prairie Dog :: A ground squirrel, named for a bark. What earns it the badge is the bark itself — the alarm calls encode the type of predator, and experiments indicate they carry information about size, colour and shape, so a colony can be told a tall human in a yellow shirt is coming. The name records the noise and misses the animal entirely.
Not What It Looks Like :: Common Eastern Firefly :: Not a fly. It is a beetle, and it does not burn — the light is a chemical reaction that produces almost no heat, which is the efficiency the name completely fails to convey. Nearly all the energy comes out as light, where a filament bulb wastes most of it as warmth.
Not What It Looks Like :: Horseshoe Crab :: Not a crab. It is closer to spiders and scorpions than to anything crustacean, and it has been that shape for around 450 million years. The name has practical consequences: it has been harvested as bait and fertiliser at a scale that would have been questioned sooner had it not been filed as a crab.
Not What It Looks Like :: Mantis Shrimp :: Not a mantis and not a shrimp. It is a stomatopod, on its own branch of the crustaceans, and the club it strikes with accelerates fast enough to boil the water in front of it — the cavitation bubble collapsing produces a second blow and a flash of light. A name that makes it sound like two harmless things.
Not What It Looks Like :: Guinea Pig :: Not from Guinea and not a pig. It is a South American rodent, domesticated in the Andes thousands of years ago as food, and it is still farmed for that across Peru and Ecuador — which makes the pet-shop name a double displacement, wrong about where it is from and about what it is for.
Not What It Looks Like :: Binturong :: Called a bearcat and neither. It is a viverrid, related to civets and genets, and it has a prehensile tail — one of only two carnivorans in the world that does. It also smells strongly of hot buttered popcorn, from a compound in its urine that happens to be one of the same molecules produced when popcorn is made.
Not What It Looks Like :: Sea Pig :: Not a pig. It is a sea cucumber that walks the abyssal plain on inflated tube feet, in herds, all facing the same way. The name is doing this badge's usual damage: it makes a genuinely strange echinoderm sound like a joke, and it is one of the most abundant large animals on the deep seafloor.
Not What It Looks Like :: Electric Eel :: Not an eel. It is a knifefish, closer to catfish than to any eel, and it delivers up to 860 volts from organs built of stacked cells that make up most of its long body. The shape is convergent; the name records the shape and misses that this is a South American freshwater fish with no relationship to eels at all.

! --- The Toolkit. Tool use, and the card says what makes each one a tool rather
! --- than a knack.
The Toolkit :: Bearded Capuchin :: The one with an archaeological record. Groups in Brazil crack palm nuts by placing them on a stone anvil and striking with a hammer stone they carry to the site, and the discarded stones build up in layers that can be excavated — the deepest go back around three thousand years. Tool use with a history, in a monkey.
The Toolkit :: Sea Otter :: It carries a favourite rock in a pouch of loose skin under the foreleg and keeps it between dives, which is the part that matters: a stone used once is opportunism, a stone kept is a tool. Individuals differ in how much they use one, and the otters that use stones most are the ones eating the hardest-shelled prey.
The Toolkit :: Common Raven :: Ravens solve multi-step problems that require ignoring the reward until the sequence is done, and they will select and keep a tool for a task they know is coming later — planning, not just using. They also cache food and re-cache it if another raven was watching, which requires modelling what the other bird saw.
The Toolkit :: Bottlenose Dolphin :: Sponging: dolphins in Shark Bay carry a marine sponge on the beak to protect it while probing the seafloor for hidden fish. The behaviour passes down the female line — daughters learn it from mothers — so it is a tool AND a tradition, which is why this animal is in Passed Down as well.
The Toolkit :: Chimpanzee :: The species that broke the definition of humans as the tool-making animal, when Jane Goodall watched one strip a grass stem to fish termites in 1960. Different populations use different kits — nut hammers here, spears for bushbabies there, moss sponges for water elsewhere — which is why the tools also count as culture.
The Toolkit :: Kea :: A mountain parrot that has been recorded using sticks to set off stoat traps, and in captivity solves puzzle boxes needing several steps in order. What sets it apart in this badge is that it does this without a strong feeding pressure to explain it: the Kea appears to work at problems for their own sake.
The Toolkit :: Satin Bowerbird :: The badge's edge case, deliberately. The male builds an avenue of sticks, decorates it with blue objects, and PAINTS the walls with chewed plant matter using a wad of bark held in the beak as a brush. The bower is not a nest and not a trap. It is a built object whose only function is to be looked at.
The Toolkit :: Sumatran Orangutan :: It makes tool SETS: a stout stick to break into a bees' nest and a finer one to extract the honey, prepared in advance. Some populations also use leaves as gloves, as umbrellas and as amplifiers — held against the mouth to lower the pitch of an alarm call and make the caller sound bigger.
The Toolkit :: Asian Elephant :: It uses a branch as a fly-swat and will modify it, breaking it to a workable length before use, which is the step that makes it tool-making rather than tool-using. Elephants have also been recorded dropping logs onto electric fences and plugging waterholes with chewed bark to stop others drinking from them.
The Toolkit :: Egyptian Vulture :: One of very few birds recorded using a tool in the wild: it picks up a stone and throws it at an ostrich egg until the shell breaks. Naive birds will do it without being taught, so the basic action appears innate rather than learned — which makes it the badge's example of a tool that is not culture.

! --- Passed Down. Culture: behaviour taught rather than inherited.
Passed Down :: Japanese Macaque :: The first documented case of culture in a non-human animal. In 1953 a young female called Imo washed sand off a sweet potato in a stream; the habit spread through the troop by watching, first to her playmates and mother, then outward, and the troop still does it — some now dip the potato in the sea between bites, apparently for the salt.
Passed Down :: Orca :: The strongest case in the badge. Groups in the same water have different diets, different calls, different hunting methods and do not interbreed — resident fish-eaters and transient mammal-hunters overlap and ignore each other. Antarctic orcas make waves to wash seals off ice floes; Patagonian ones strand deliberately on beaches, and mothers teach it. Culture strong enough to be splitting a species.
Passed Down :: Chimpanzee :: Neighbouring populations with the same anatomy and the same forest do different things — nut-cracking in one place and not the next, different grooming handclasps, different termite techniques — and the differences track social contact rather than genetics or habitat. That comparison across sites is the standard evidence for animal culture.
Passed Down :: Bottlenose Dolphin :: Sponging passes down the female line in Shark Bay, and spongers associate preferentially with other spongers — a subgroup defined by a learned skill rather than by kinship alone. Dolphins also have signature whistles, invented in the first months of life, that others copy to address them by.
Passed Down :: Sperm Whale :: Clans defined by dialect. Sperm whales across the Pacific belong to clans identified by their patterns of clicks, and clans with different codas have different movement and foraging behaviour and do not mix — even sharing the same water. A whale's clan is learned from the group it grows up in.
Passed Down :: Humpback Whale :: Song that spreads like a fashion. Every male in a population sings the same song, it changes through a season, and a new song arriving from a neighbouring population can sweep across an entire ocean basin within a few years, replacing the old one completely. It has been tracked moving west to east across the South Pacific.
Passed Down :: Meerkat :: Teaching, in the strict sense, which is rarer than culture. Adults bring scorpions to pups and modify what they bring by the pup's age — dead first, then disabled with the sting removed, then live — at a cost to themselves and with no benefit except the pup learning. Very few animals have been shown to do that.

! --- Strange Allies. Cooperation between unrelated species.
Strange Allies :: Greater Honeyguide :: The only partnership in this badge where the other species is US. The bird leads human honey-hunters to a bees' nest, answering a specific call its partners have used for generations — in Mozambique the right call roughly triples the chance of success — and it takes the wax after the people have taken the honey. Neither can do it alone: the bird cannot open the nest, and the people cannot find it.
Strange Allies :: Pistol Shrimp :: The shrimp digs and the fish watches. A nearly blind pistol shrimp excavates a burrow both animals live in, keeping one antenna in contact with a goby at the entrance; the goby flicks its tail to signal danger and both retreat. The shrimp does the labour, the fish does the seeing, and neither is related to the other.
Strange Allies :: Bluestreak Cleaner Wrasse :: A cleaning station is a truce. Predators queue, hold still, and open their mouths and gills to a fish small enough to eat — and do not. Removing the cleaners from a patch of reef reduces the number and diversity of other fish there, so the arrangement holds the community together rather than just tidying it.
Strange Allies :: Percula Clownfish :: The classic mutualism and the mechanism is still argued over — the clownfish is protected from the anemone's sting by a mucus coat, acquired partly by acclimating to that anemone. What it gives back is measurable: it drives off polyp-eaters, and its movements ventilate the anemone at night, so the anemone grows faster with a fish in it.
Strange Allies :: Giant Green Anemone :: Its green is partly its own protein and partly the algae living inside its tissues, which photosynthesise and pass sugars to their host. A 2024 study found the algae are not the main source of the colour, which is worth saying — the partnership is real, and the neat old explanation of it was too simple.
Strange Allies :: Venus Flower Basket :: A partnership that is also a prison. A pair of shrimp enter the glass sponge as larvae, grow too large to leave, and spend their lives inside — cleaning it and being sheltered and fed by the current it filters. Their offspring leave to find a sponge of their own. In Japan a dried skeleton with the pair still inside was a wedding gift.
Strange Allies :: Banded Sea Krait :: A venomous sea snake that hunts cooperatively with fish that are not its relatives. It flushes prey out of crevices in the reef, and trevally and goatfish follow it to take whatever escapes — a hunting association between a reptile and fish, each getting at prey the other's method cannot reach.
Strange Allies :: Banded Mongoose :: Mongooses and warthogs. Warthogs lie down and let banded mongooses climb over them to pick off ticks, and both parties seek the encounter out. It is grooming across the mammal orders, initiated by an animal being groomed that could easily leave, which is what makes it cooperation rather than one species exploiting another.

! --- The Impostor. Mimicry.
The Impostor :: Mimic Octopus :: The only animal known to impersonate MULTIPLE other species and choose between them. It flattens and undulates as a flatfish, trails arms as a lionfish, or hides six arms and waves two as a banded sea snake — and there is evidence it picks the sea snake when harassed by damselfish, which sea snakes eat. Selecting the right disguise for the audience.
The Impostor :: Orchid Mantis :: It does not mimic an orchid. Its lobed pink legs make a flower-like shape that attracts pollinating insects better than actual flowers do in the same habitat — tests found insects preferred the mantis. It is a lure rather than camouflage, and the model does not exist: it is a generalised flower nobody has to recognise.
The Impostor :: Ornate Ghost Pipefish :: It hangs head-down and matches whatever it is living in — a crinoid, a gorgonian, a clump of drifting weed — with fringed body appendages that break its outline into the same texture. It changes colour to fit the host over a few days, so an individual's disguise is specific to the object it settled on.
The Impostor :: Marmalade Hoverfly :: Batesian mimicry: harmless and dressed as dangerous. It has the black and yellow bands of a wasp and no sting at all, and the disguise works only while wasps remain common enough for predators to have learned the lesson. It also crosses Europe in migrations of billions and pollinates while doing it.
The Impostor :: Giant Leaf Insect :: The disguise includes the damage. It is leaf-shaped with leaf venation, and it is also marked with false brown patches that look like fungal spots and holes chewed by other insects — mimicking a leaf that has been eaten. And it rocks as it walks, so a moving insect reads as a leaf in wind.
The Impostor :: Superb Lyrebird :: Vocal mimicry, and the most accurate there is: it reproduces the calls of other birds so exactly that recordings fool the species being copied. Wild lyrebirds have been recorded imitating chainsaws and camera shutters, though the famous documentary examples came from birds that had lived near people. Males mimic during display, and the size of the repertoire is what females are judging.
The Impostor :: Great Potoo :: It does not hide, it becomes furniture. By day it perches upright on a broken branch stub, extends its head and neck into a line with the stump, and stays there — and it can watch through slits in its closed eyelids without opening its eyes. The disguise depends on not reacting no matter how close an observer gets.
The Impostor :: Tawny Frogmouth :: The same broken-branch trick as the great potoo in this badge, on a different continent and in an unrelated family — and constantly mistaken for an owl, which it is not. It stretches upright with the bill raised and the eyes almost shut, and people walk past it. Two lineages arriving at the same posture is what puts both in this badge together.

! --- Who's That. Mirror self-recognition. The card has to be careful: the test is
! --- contested, and a card that overclaims is exactly this project's failure mode.
Who's That :: Asian Elephant :: One of the few large mammals to pass the mark test, in 2006, and it needed a mirror big enough — earlier failures used mirrors too small for the animal to see itself in. An elephant that has passed will use the mirror to inspect the inside of its own mouth, which is the behaviour that convinces people the recognition is real.
Who's That :: Bottlenose Dolphin :: Passed without hands, which is why the test had to be redesigned. A dolphin cannot touch a mark, so the evidence is that a marked animal swims straight to the mirror and positions itself to view the marked part. Young dolphins show self-directed behaviour at a mirror earlier than human children do.
Who's That :: Orca :: Tested with the same approach as the bottlenose dolphin beside it, and it belongs in this badge for the same reason it belongs in Passed Down: this is an animal with lifelong group membership, learned dialects and a name-like signature. Self-recognition is one part of a mind built around knowing exactly who everybody is.
Who's That :: Chimpanzee :: The species the test was invented on, by Gordon Gallup in 1970. Marked while anaesthetised, a chimpanzee wakes, sees the mirror, and touches the mark on its own head rather than the glass. Every other animal in this badge is being compared against what this one did.
Who's That :: Bonobo :: Passes as chimpanzees do, and it is here as the pair rather than the duplicate: the two are each other's closest relatives, split by the Congo River, and where they differ is in temperament rather than in this ability. Their behaviour at a mirror is a shared inheritance rather than two independent arrivals.
Who's That :: Sumatran Orangutan :: Passes, and does so in the most leisurely way of any ape — using the mirror to examine parts of itself it cannot otherwise see, over long unhurried sessions. Gorillas mostly do not pass, which is the awkward gap in the great-ape story and is usually put down to their avoiding eye contact rather than to what they understand.
Who's That :: Eurasian Magpie :: The result that broke the mammal monopoly, in 2008: marked birds scratched at their own throats rather than at the glass, and stopped once the mark was gone. Later attempts to replicate it have had mixed results, and that is worth saying — a famous finding that is being re-tested is science working, not science failing.
Who's That :: Giant Oceanic Manta :: It has the largest brain of any fish, and in a 2016 study it repeatedly approached a mirror, made unusual repetitive movements in front of it, and blew bubbles — behaviour it did not show towards another manta. It did not take a mark test. The card says so: this is contingency-checking, which is suggestive and not the same as passing.
Who's That :: Bluestreak Cleaner Wrasse :: The most contested entry in the badge and the most interesting. In 2019 cleaner wrasse were reported scraping a marked throat against surfaces after seeing themselves — passing the mark test at a body size and brain size nobody expected. The argument since is whether the fish recognises itself or is responding to something that looks like a parasite. The paper's own authors say it forces the question of what the test measures.
Who's That :: Horse :: Reported to pass a mark test in 2021 — horses touched a cross drawn on their cheek only when they could see it in a mirror. It is a recent and less-replicated result than the apes above it, and its interest is that a herd animal with sideways-set eyes and no hands is a very different mind to be finding this in.

! --- The Village. Cooperative breeding.
The Village :: Meerkat :: The textbook case. A dominant pair breeds and everybody else babysits, teaches, and stands sentry — and the cost is real: sentries eat less, and subordinate females who breed anyway are often evicted or have their pups killed. Cooperative breeding is not simply kindly; it is enforced.
The Village :: Banded Mongoose :: The most striking birth-timing in any mammal: every breeding female in a group gives birth on the SAME DAY, usually the same night. Because the pups are mixed and nobody can tell whose is whose, mothers cannot target other females' young — the synchrony is what stops the infanticide.
The Village :: African Wild Dog :: One breeding pair, and the whole pack raises the litter — including regurgitating meat for pups, for the mother, and for injured or elderly adults who did not make the kill. A pack votes on when to move by sneezing, and the threshold needed is lower when a dominant animal starts it.
The Village :: Naked Mole-Rat :: Cooperative breeding taken to its endpoint: one queen who breeds and a colony of non-breeding helpers who dig, forage, care for pups and defend. It is the only mammal that is genuinely eusocial, which is why it is in The Colony too — the difference between the two badges is precisely how far this can be pushed.
The Village :: Pygmy Marmoset :: Twins are the norm and they are heavy relative to the mother, so she cannot carry them alone. The father and older siblings do most of the carrying, handing the infants back only to nurse. Cooperative breeding here is not optional help — the reproductive strategy is built assuming it.
The Village :: Golden Tamarin :: The same shared-carrying system as the pygmy marmoset, in a species where it had to be relearned in captivity: early reintroduction attempts failed partly because zoo-raised animals had never watched anyone else raise infants. The programme had to let family groups form and teach each other before releases worked.
The Village :: Dhole :: An Asian wild dog whose packs let pups eat FIRST at a kill, ahead of the adults who made it — the reverse of most social carnivores. Non-breeding adults regurgitate for the litter and guard the den, and packs will merge into large temporary groups, which is unusual for a canid.

! --- The Long Way. Migration extremes.
The Long Way :: Arctic Tern :: The longest migration of any animal — tracking gives round trips of seventy to ninety thousand kilometres a year, because it does not fly straight but follows the wind. It breeds in the Arctic summer and winters in the Antarctic one, so it sees more daylight in a lifetime than anything else alive.
The Long Way :: Globe Skimmer :: The longest migration of any insect: a round trip of roughly eighteen thousand kilometres across the Indian Ocean, made in generations, with each dragonfly flying one leg and the next generation continuing. It rides the monsoon at altitude, breeds in temporary pools, and is found on every continent except Antarctica.
The Long Way :: Bar-tailed Godwit :: The longest non-stop flight recorded for any bird: a tagged bird flew from Alaska to Tasmania in eleven days without landing, eating or drinking — over thirteen thousand kilometres. It shrinks its own digestive organs before departure to save weight and rebuilds them on arrival.
The Long Way :: Humpback Whale :: One of the longest mammal migrations, up to about eight thousand kilometres each way between polar feeding grounds and tropical breeding waters — and it fasts for most of it, living on fat laid down in one season to pay for the other. Mothers make the journey while nursing.
The Long Way :: Monarch Butterfly :: The one where no individual completes the round trip. It takes three or four generations to travel north through the summer, and then a single long-lived generation flies the whole way back to a small area of Mexican forest it has never seen, guided by a sun compass and the earth's magnetic field.
The Long Way :: Leatherback Turtle :: The widest-ranging reptile there is, crossing entire ocean basins between nesting beaches and jellyfish feeding grounds, and swimming in water far too cold for any other turtle — it holds its body above ambient temperature by sheer size, insulation and heat exchangers in the flippers.
The Long Way :: Reindeer :: The longest land migration of any mammal — some herds cover well over a thousand kilometres a year — and it is here for what that means when a route is blocked. Roads, pipelines and fences cut migrations that are learned and traditional, and a herd that loses a route does not simply take another.
The Long Way :: Wildebeest :: The largest land migration by weight of animals: over a million animals moving in a rough circuit through the Serengeti and Mara, following rain they appear to detect at a distance. The system depends on the whole circuit staying open, which is why fencing a single section can collapse a migration that has run for millennia.

! --- The Long Sleep. Hibernation and torpor. The card should keep the two apart
! --- where the distinction is the interesting part.
The Long Sleep :: Alpine Marmot :: Eight months underground, in a family heap with the young in the middle — and that arrangement is the point. Marmot pups cannot store enough fat to survive alone, so the adults' body heat carries them through; a litter without adults in the burrow does not come out in spring. Hibernation done socially.
The Long Sleep :: Black Bear :: The badge's argument about definitions. A bear's body temperature drops only a few degrees, far less than a true hibernator's, but it goes months without eating, drinking, urinating or defecating and loses almost no muscle or bone — and it gives birth in the middle of it. Whether that counts as hibernation is a naming question; what it does is more remarkable than the label.
The Long Sleep :: Sword-billed Hummingbird :: Not hibernation but nightly torpor, which is the other half of this badge. A hummingbird burns so fast it cannot survive a cold night at normal metabolism, so it drops its body temperature close to ambient and effectively switches off until morning. It does this every night of its life rather than once a year.
The Long Sleep :: Edible Dormouse :: The longest hibernation of any mammal — up to eleven months. In a year when the beech and oak fail to produce a good crop it may not breed at all and simply stay underground, so its calendar is set by the trees rather than by the temperature.
The Long Sleep :: Groundhog :: A true hibernator, with body temperature falling to a few degrees above freezing and the heart down to a handful of beats a minute — which makes the folklore ridiculous in a useful way. A groundhog in early February is deep in hibernation and in no condition to look at anything, let alone its own shadow.
The Long Sleep :: European Hedgehog :: It wakes repeatedly through the winter — hibernation is not continuous, and every arousal is expensive, so a hedgehog that is disturbed too often runs out of fat before spring. That is why bonfire piles and tidied gardens matter to it: the cost is not the cold, it is being woken.
The Long Sleep :: Tawny Frogmouth :: The badge's bird that does it in the cold rather than overnight for energy. Frogmouths enter torpor on cold mornings, dropping body temperature and staying immobile — which combines with the broken-branch posture that puts them in The Impostor. The disguise and the energy saving are the same behaviour.
The Long Sleep :: Little Brown Bat :: Hibernates in caves for six or seven months, in colonies, at a heart rate of a few beats a minute. It is also the animal that shows what the state costs in risk: white-nose syndrome kills by rousing bats repeatedly through winter so they burn their fat and starve, and it has removed over ninety percent of them in much of eastern North America.
The Long Sleep :: Eastern Chipmunk :: It does not fatten up — it stores food instead, and wakes every few days through the winter to eat from a larder in the burrow. That is a different strategy from every other mammal in this badge, and it is why its cheek pouches matter: the hibernation is paid for in autumn by carrying nearly its own body weight in seed underground.

! --- Bad Reputation. Feared for the name, the face or the folklore - and fear
! --- kills them. The card should name the fear and then answer it.
Bad Reputation :: Vampire Squid :: Named for a resemblance and nothing it does. It drinks no blood and hunts nothing: it trails two filaments through the water and collects marine snow — the slow fall of dead matter from above — and rolls it into balls with mucus. Its Latin name means "vampire squid from hell", which is a naturalist's joke that stuck to a detritivore.
Bad Reputation :: Zombie Worm :: Feared for the name, which is the badge category it sits in most exactly. Its published common name is "bone-eating snot flower". It lives only on sunken whale skeletons, has no mouth and no gut, and dissolves bone with acid so symbiotic bacteria can reach the fat inside — a recycler, named as if it were a horror.
Bad Reputation :: Hellbender :: A giant salamander with a folk reputation for being venomous, slimy and destructive to fishing, none of which is true — it is harmless, and it is an indicator of clean, cold, well-oxygenated water. Anglers killed them on sight for generations. Its populations are now collapsing from siltation, and the reputation slowed anyone caring.
Bad Reputation :: Common Basilisk :: Named after a mythological creature whose gaze killed, and it is a small lizard that eats insects. What it actually does is more interesting than the myth: it runs across water on its hind legs, slapping the surface fast enough to create air pockets before it sinks, for twenty metres or so.
Bad Reputation :: Thorny Devil :: It looks like a weapon and it eats ants — nothing else, up to a few thousand a day, one at a time. The spines are defensive and the false head on the back of its neck is a decoy it presents while tucking the real one down. Its grooved skin also draws water to its mouth by capillary action from any damp sand it stands on.
Bad Reputation :: Tasmanian Devil :: Named for a scream in the dark that terrified early settlers, and it is a carrion specialist that mostly eats things already dead. The screaming is a squabble over a carcass, not aggression toward anything larger. It was persecuted with bounties for a century, and what threatens it now is a transmissible facial cancer, not people.
Bad Reputation :: Giant Oceanic Manta :: "Devil ray" — the cephalic fins on the head, which it uses to funnel plankton toward its mouth, were read as horns. It has no sting, no teeth to speak of, and the largest brain of any fish. It is also the badge member that shows what the fear costs: for decades they were killed as pests before dive tourism made a living one worth more.
Bad Reputation :: Goblin Shark :: Feared for the face, and the face is a mechanism. The jaw is not fixed in that lunging position — it slingshots forward to catch prey and retracts, and it only looks like that in photographs taken at the moment of a strike or after death. The long flat snout is packed with electroreceptors for hunting in the dark.
Bad Reputation :: Maned Wolf :: Persecuted across South America on folklore — that parts of it cure illness, that it takes livestock — and its diet is about half fruit. It is shy, it does not form packs, and its main threat now is roads and the loss of the grassland it walks through. A shape people found sinister on an animal that mostly eats a tomato relative.
Bad Reputation :: Aye-aye :: The clearest case in the badge of a reputation killing an animal. In parts of Madagascar it is an omen of death, and one seen near a village may be killed on sight — so the fear is not a side issue, it is the threat. The long middle finger that makes it look uncanny is a feeding tool for extracting grubs.
Bad Reputation :: Humpback Anglerfish :: Feared for a photograph. It is about twenty centimetres long, and every terrifying image of it is a magnified close-up of a small, soft-bodied fish that lives where there is nothing to eat and cannot afford to let anything pass. The teeth and the gape are for a life with one meal in a long while.
Bad Reputation :: Common Fangtooth :: The largest teeth of any fish relative to its head, on an animal sixteen centimetres long. It cannot close its mouth over them, which is why the skull has sockets beside the brain for the lower fangs to slide into. Everything frightening about it is scaled to something that would fit in a hand.
Bad Reputation :: Gulper Eel :: A mouth bigger than the rest of the animal, hinged far back, which reads as a monster and appears to be a net. Its stomach contents are mostly small crustaceans — the enormous jaw is probably for sweeping up a great deal of very little rather than for swallowing anything large.
Bad Reputation :: Blobfish :: Voted the world's ugliest animal on the strength of a corpse. At the depth it lives, under about a hundred atmospheres, it is an ordinary-looking fish; its soft bones and gelatinous flesh let it hold position without a swim bladder. Brought to the surface, the pressure that gave it shape is gone and the face collapses. The famous photograph is a decompression injury.
Bad Reputation :: Giant Isopod :: A woodlouse relative at half a metre, which people find horrifying for reasons of scale alone. It is a scavenger on a seafloor where food arrives rarely and all at once, so it is built to gorge and then wait — one in a Japanese aquarium refused every meal offered for over five years and stayed apparently healthy.
Bad Reputation :: Pacific Hagfish :: Feared for the slime and for eating the dead, and both are true and neither is the point. It turns a bucket of seawater into gel in seconds as a defence that chokes a predator's gills, then ties itself in a knot to scrape it off. Its slime threads are stronger than nylon for their weight and are being studied as a material.
Bad Reputation :: Giant Moray :: The open mouth that looks like a threat is the animal breathing — a moray must gape to pump water over its gills, so its resting face is a snarl it cannot help. Bites happen when a hand goes into a hole, which is a hand's mistake. It hunts cooperatively with groupers, which is not the behaviour of a monster.
Bad Reputation :: Common Raven :: Feared for folklore across most of Europe — an omen, a battlefield bird, a witch's companion — and it is one of the most intelligent animals alive, with planning, deception and play. The association with death comes from doing a scavenger's job well. Ravens also gather at wolf kills and appear to lead wolves to carcasses they cannot open.
Bad Reputation :: Barn Owl :: The white face in the dark that produced screaming-woman folklore across half the world, and it is a silent, harmless hunter of rodents. Its feathers have a comb-like leading edge and a velvet surface that kills the sound of flight, so it hears its prey and its prey does not hear it. Farmers who once nailed them to barn doors now build boxes for them.
Bad Reputation :: Grey Wolf :: The animal this badge is really about. Folklore, bounties and government programmes removed it from most of its range, and the science used to justify the fear — the alpha-wolf hierarchy — came from unrelated captives and has been retracted by the man who popularised it. A wild pack is a family. The reputation outlived the evidence by fifty years.
Bad Reputation :: Spotted Hyena :: Called a cowardly scavenger, and it hunts most of what it eats — lions scavenge from hyenas more often than the reverse. It lives in matriarchal clans with structured politics, and its bone-crushing jaws recycle what nothing else can digest. Almost every element of its reputation is the opposite of the animal.
Bad Reputation :: Common Vampire Bat :: It does drink blood, which is why it is the honest entry here. What the fear misses is the reciprocity: a bat that fails to feed will die within about seventy hours, and its roost-mates regurgitate blood to save it — preferentially for bats that have fed them before. It is one of the clearest cases of food-sharing between unrelated animals.
Bad Reputation :: Rüppell's Vulture :: Feared and despised as a carrion bird, and it is the highest-flying bird ever recorded — one struck an aircraft at over eleven thousand metres. It is also Critically Endangered, down by more than ninety percent, largely from poisoned carcasses. Vultures remove disease from a landscape, and where they have gone, rabies and anthrax have gone up.

! --- Only In Captivity. The membership is exactly the EW tag in design/TAGS.txt,
! --- and Uncle Albert checks that the two match. Three of these are tagged as a
! --- population rather than an IUCN species listing, and the card says so.
Only In Captivity :: Spix's Macaw :: The one everybody knows from the film, and the film left out the arithmetic. By 1990 there was exactly one male left in the wild, and he paired with a female of another species for years because there was nothing else. Every Spix's macaw alive descends from birds taken illegally for the trade — the collectors who emptied the wild are the reason there is a captive population to release from.
Only In Captivity :: Guam Kingfisher :: Extinct in the wild since the 1980s, and every living bird descends from 29 taken into captivity as the brown tree snake worked through Guam's forest. Releases began on a snake-free island in 2024, thirty-eight years after the last wild bird — long enough that no bird alive had ever seen the habitat it was bred for.
Only In Captivity :: Socorro Dove :: Gone from Socorro Island by 1972, to cats and to grazing that stripped the understorey, and it survived only because a few birds sat in European and American private collections. The reintroduction has had to wait on removing the cats and regrowing the forest — the bird was the easy part to keep.
Only In Captivity :: ʻAlalā :: The Hawaiian crow, a tool-user, extinct in the wild since 2002. The hard part has not been breeding it but releasing it: birds put back kept being killed by the ʻio, the native Hawaiian hawk, which they had lost any experience of. Later cohorts were given anti-predator training before release, which is a strange sentence to have to write about a wild species.
Only In Captivity :: Kihansi Spray Toad :: Its entire world was the spray zone of one waterfall in Tanzania, a few hectares, kept permanently wet by the mist. A dam took the water and the mist went with it. Animals had been taken to American zoos just beforehand, and the species now exists in tanks and in a sprinkler-fed patch of gorge — a habitat that has to be switched on.
Only In Captivity :: Wyoming Toad :: Down to a handful of animals by the mid-1980s after chytrid fungus, pesticide and habitat loss, and it has been bred and released for decades into a small set of Wyoming wetlands where survival past the first winter remains poor. It is the badge's illustration that captive breeding solves the supply of animals, not the reason they died.
Only In Captivity :: Père David's Deer :: The one where the captivity happened by accident and centuries early. Extinct in China, it survived because a herd sat in an English park at Woburn — animals sent to Europe in the nineteenth century, before anyone thought of that as conservation. Reintroduced herds now live in China and the listing has not moved.
Only In Captivity :: Barbary Lion :: Tagged here with a caveat that matters: it is a POPULATION of Panthera leo, not a species. The lion as a species is Vulnerable and in no danger of vanishing; the North African population is gone from the wild and survives only as captive descendants, some traced to royal collections in Morocco. The badge concept is exactly true of it, and the taxonomy is not.
Only In Captivity :: Northern White Rhino :: The starkest entry in the game. It is a subspecies, not a species — the southern white rhino numbers in the thousands — and there are two females left, neither able to carry a calf. Its survival now depends on stored eggs, frozen sperm and a southern white surrogate. Functionally extinct in the wild rather than formally listed as such.
Only In Captivity :: South China Tiger :: Listed Critically Endangered rather than Extinct in the Wild, with no confirmed wild animal in over thirty years. It is tagged here because the badge's concept — nowhere left to go home to — is plainly true of it, and pretending the line is cleaner than it is would teach the wrong thing. Every animal is descended from six wild-caught tigers.
Only In Captivity :: Partula nodosa :: A Polynesian tree snail wiped from Tahiti by an introduced predatory snail that had itself been introduced to control an introduced pest. London Zoo and partners kept it alive in cupboards for forty years, and since 2015 over twenty thousand have gone back into fenced valleys — each marked with a dot of UV paint so surveyors can find them at night.

! --- Back From the Brink. Recoveries that worked, and the card says what the
! --- intervention actually was.
Back From the Brink :: California Condor :: In 1987 every remaining condor — twenty-seven birds — was taken into captivity, which was bitterly opposed at the time as giving up on the wild. There are now several hundred flying. Chicks are still reared with hand puppets to avoid imprinting, and lead poisoning from ammunition in carcasses remains the thing that would undo it.
Back From the Brink :: Przewalski's Horse :: The only never-domesticated horse left, extinct in the wild by the 1960s and rebuilt from twelve founder animals in zoos. Released back onto the Mongolian steppe from the 1990s, it is now downlisted from Extinct in the Wild to Endangered. The genetic bottleneck it came through is permanent and is the recovery's long-term problem.
Back From the Brink :: Whooping Crane :: Down to about fifteen birds in the 1940s. What brought it back included teaching a new migration route from scratch — captive-reared cranes following an ultralight aircraft south — because the knowledge of where to go is learned from adults and there were no adults left who knew.
Back From the Brink :: Mauritius Kestrel :: Four known birds in 1974, one breeding pair, and it is often called the rarest bird there has ever been. Carl Jones took eggs, hatched them, and put chicks back — against advice to spend the money elsewhere. There are now several hundred. It is why this species sits here and not in a memorial.
Back From the Brink :: Arabian Oryx :: The first species ever downlisted from Extinct in the Wild, in 2011. It was hunted out of the wild by 1972, rebuilt from nine animals in what was called Operation Oryx, and released back into Oman and neighbouring countries. It is now listed Vulnerable, which is why it is not in On the Brink at all.
Back From the Brink :: American Bison :: From tens of millions to a few hundred wild animals inside a single human lifetime, and back to hundreds of thousands. The recovery is real and it is incomplete in a way worth stating: most bison alive are managed as livestock and many carry cattle genes, so the animal came back more securely than the wild population did.
Back From the Brink :: Golden Tamarin :: Down to a few hundred in fragments of Brazilian Atlantic forest. The recovery needed captive breeding, reintroduction, and forest CORRIDORS planted to reconnect the fragments — without the corridors the released groups were still isolated. Roughly a third of the wild population descends from zoo-bred animals.
Back From the Brink :: Black-footed Ferret :: Declared extinct twice. A small population found in Wyoming in 1981 was then almost wiped out by disease, and everything alive today descends from seven animals. It has since been cloned from decades-old frozen cells to add genetic diversity back — the first native endangered species in the United States cloned for that purpose.
Back From the Brink :: Iberian Lynx :: About ninety-four animals in 2002, past two thousand by 2023, and moved from Endangered to Vulnerable in 2024. Saving the cat meant first restoring its food: it eats little but European rabbit, and the rabbit had been emptied out by two introduced diseases. Warrens were rebuilt and underpasses dug beneath the roads that were killing lynxes fastest.

! --- The Linchpin. Keystone species. The card must say what falls over, because
! --- "important" is not the claim.
The Linchpin :: Eurasian Beaver :: It does not live in the landscape so much as build it. Dams slow water, spread it sideways, raise the water table and create wetland that holds carbon and fish and slows floods downstream — and when beavers were removed from Europe those wetlands drained. Reintroductions in Britain are measured in flood peaks and water quality, not in beavers.
The Linchpin :: Sea Otter :: The original marine keystone. Otters eat sea urchins; without otters the urchins graze kelp holdfasts and the forest becomes a barren, taking everything that lived in it. Fur hunting removed the otters and the barrens followed. It is the clearest case in the badge because the mechanism was watched running both directions.
The Linchpin :: Grey Wolf :: The Yellowstone story, told carefully. Wolves returned in 1995, elk behaviour and numbers changed, and willow and aspen recovered in places — but the popular version, in which wolves changed the course of the rivers, overstates a contested result. Later work argues bears, drought and human hunting all contributed. The wolf matters here; it is not the only thing that moved.
The Linchpin :: Stoplight Parrotfish :: It eats the algae that would otherwise smother coral, and excretes the rock it scrapes as sand. Reefs where parrotfish are fished out shift to algae and stay there, so protecting this one species is a standard reef intervention — Bonaire and Belize both banned taking them, and the reefs responded.
The Linchpin :: African Elephant :: An ecosystem engineer at a scale nothing else manages: it pushes over trees and keeps savanna from closing into woodland, digs waterholes in dry riverbeds that everything else drinks from, and disperses large seeds nothing else can swallow whole. Remove it and the vegetation structure changes, which changes everything living in it.
The Linchpin :: Prairie Dog :: Its burrow systems aerate soil and change what grows, and its colonies are the habitat other species require — the black-footed ferret in this badge's neighbour eats almost nothing else and dens in prairie dog holes. Poisoning campaigns removed over ninety percent of them, and the ferret nearly went with them.
The Linchpin :: American Bison :: Grazing, wallowing and trampling maintained the shortgrass prairie, and the wallows themselves held water and became distinct micro-habitats. Cattle do not do the same job: they graze differently, they are fenced, and they do not wallow. The prairie that remains is shaped by an animal that mostly is not on it any more.
The Linchpin :: Ochre Sea Star :: The animal the whole concept was defined on. Robert Paine removed it from a stretch of Washington shore in the 1960s and the mussels took over, crowding out most other species — and he coined "keystone species" for what he had just demonstrated. Everything else in this badge is being described with a word invented for this starfish.
The Linchpin :: Sunflower Sea Star :: A second urchin predator alongside the sea otter, and its collapse is a live experiment nobody wanted. Sea star wasting disease removed it from most of its range from 2013, urchin numbers rose sharply, and kelp forests off northern California fell by around ninety percent. The keystone role became visible by being subtracted.
The Linchpin :: Purple Sea Urchin :: The one that is not a keystone, and is here because the badge does not work without it. It is the lever the otter and the sunflower star press on: released from both predators it multiplies, grazes the kelp to bare rock, and then persists in a starved, low-value state for years. The keystone species matter because of what this animal does when they go.

! --- The Trade. The most trafficked animals on earth.
The Trade :: Sunda Pangolin :: The most trafficked wild mammal in the world. Scales are keratin — chemically the same as fingernails — and are used in traditional medicine with no demonstrated effect, and the meat is a luxury. Seizures are measured in tonnes. Its only defence is to roll into a ball, which against a person is not a defence but a way of being picked up.
The Trade :: Tiger :: Trafficked in pieces: bones for wine, skins for display, and everything else for medicine. There are now more tigers in captivity in the United States and in Asian tiger farms than survive in the wild, and the farms are the argument — whether a legal supply relieves pressure on wild animals or launders them, which is still unresolved.
The Trade :: Black Rhinoceros :: Reduced by around ninety-eight percent between 1960 and the mid-1990s for a horn made of keratin. It has recovered somewhat and is still Critically Endangered. Rangers now dehorn animals as a deterrent, which is a measure of how bad this is: the species is being disfigured to make it worthless.
The Trade :: Saiga :: An antelope of the Central Asian steppe with a strange inflated nose that filters dust, hunted for horn after the collapse of the Soviet Union removed the enforcement overnight — over ninety-five percent gone in about fifteen years. Only males have horns, so the hunting skewed the sex ratio until females could not find mates.
The Trade :: African Grey :: The most trafficked parrot there is, and the reason is exactly what makes it valuable in the first place: it talks, and Alex demonstrated it could do far more than repeat. Ghana lost around ninety-nine percent of its population in a few decades. It was moved to CITES Appendix I in 2016, banning commercial international trade.
The Trade :: Slow Loris :: Trafficked because of internet video. The clips of a loris being tickled with its arms up show a defensive posture, not enjoyment, and it is the only venomous primate — so traffickers cut its teeth out with clippers before sale, which frequently kills it. Demand created by footage of an animal being distressed.
The Trade :: Ball Python :: The most exported live African snake there has been, in the millions, much of it under a "captive-bred" label that covers eggs taken from wild females and hatched in sheds. It is here as the legal end of the badge: a trade that is largely lawful, enormous, and still removing animals from the wild at scale.
The Trade :: Hyacinth Macaw :: The largest flying parrot, and in the 1980s at least ten thousand were taken from the wild for the pet trade — a substantial fraction of everything alive. It nests almost exclusively in the hollows of one tree species and depends on a toucan to disperse the tree's seeds, which is why the recovery has needed nest boxes as well as enforcement.
The Trade :: Spix's Macaw :: The endpoint of this badge, and it is in Only In Captivity for the same reason. Trapping for collectors, not habitat loss, is what took the last wild birds — and the private collections that resulted are what the reintroduction had to be built from. The trade destroyed the species and then held the only copies.

! --- The Largest. The card says largest of WHAT, because that is the badge.
The Largest :: Blue Whale :: The largest animal that has ever lived, as far as anyone knows — larger than any dinosaur, up to about thirty metres and 150 tonnes. Its tongue alone can weigh as much as an elephant, and a newborn calf gains around ninety kilograms a day on milk. Nothing has ever been bigger, and it eats some of the smallest animals in the sea.
The Largest :: African Elephant :: The largest land animal. Everything about it is a consequence of that: legs like columns with a fatty pad in the foot to spread the load, ears used as radiators because a large body sheds heat badly, and a trunk because a neck strong enough to hold that skull cannot reach the ground.
The Largest :: Whale Shark :: The largest fish, up to about eighteen metres — and a filter feeder, like the blue whale beside it. That is the pattern the badge keeps producing: the biggest animals in the water are almost never predators, because straining the ocean scales up better than chasing things does.
The Largest :: Ostrich :: The largest living bird, and the largest egg of any bird alive — though relative to the mother it is one of the smallest. It cannot fly, and it does not need to: it runs at about seventy kilometres an hour on two toes, which is why it is in The Fastest as well.
The Largest :: Japanese Spider Crab :: The largest leg span of any arthropod, up to about three and a half metres. Growth requires shedding the whole external skeleton, which gets harder and riskier the larger the animal is — this one gets away with it by living deep in cold water where its own weight is largely supported.
The Largest :: Giant Siphonophore :: The badge's difficult entry: it is measured in tens of metres and it is not one animal in the ordinary sense. It grows from a single fertilised egg into a chain of specialised bodies that cannot live apart, which raises the question of whether "largest" should be measured by the individual or by the thing you can see.
The Largest :: Saltwater Crocodile :: The largest living reptile, up to about six metres and a tonne, and it has the strongest bite ever measured in a living animal. The muscles that OPEN the jaw are weak enough that a person can hold them shut — all the power is in one direction, which is the only direction that matters.
The Largest :: Colossal Squid :: The largest invertebrate by mass — heavier than the giant squid and shorter, which is the comparison people usually get backwards. It also carries the largest eyes of any animal that has ever lived, about twenty-seven centimetres, thought to be for seeing a sperm whale approaching in the dark.
The Largest :: Capybara :: The largest rodent, at up to about sixty-five kilograms, and it is here to show where the ceiling on a body plan sits. Rodents are limited by ever-growing incisors and a small-mammal digestive system; the capybara pushes both as far as they go by being semi-aquatic and eating grass, and nothing rodent-shaped has done better.
The Largest :: Goliath Frog :: The largest frog, over three kilograms and thirty centimetres of body. It has no vocal sac and does not call — it whistles and builds nest ponds by moving stones, some weighing as much as itself. Its size and its restricted range in Cameroon and Equatorial Guinea are also why it is Endangered.
The Largest :: Goliath Beetle :: Among the heaviest insects, up to about a hundred grams as a larva. The limit is breathing: insects have no lungs and take oxygen through tubes that carry it directly to the tissue, which stops working well past a certain body thickness. That is the ceiling this beetle is pressed against.
The Largest :: Ocean Sunfish :: The heaviest bony fish, up to over two tonnes — and it starts as a larva a couple of millimetres long, a size increase of about sixty million times, the largest of any vertebrate. It looks like a fish that stops halfway because the tail fin never develops; it swims with the dorsal and anal fins instead.
The Largest :: Oarfish :: The longest bony fish, recorded past eight metres and reported longer. It swims vertically, head up, undulating a dorsal fin that runs its whole length, and it is the origin of a good deal of sea-serpent folklore — the animals people find are usually dying ones that have come to the surface.
The Largest :: Wandering Albatross :: The largest wingspan of any living bird, up to about three and a half metres, and the wings are built for not flapping: a tendon locks the joint so the bird can hold them out with almost no muscular effort and ride wind for hours. It can cover a thousand kilometres in a day.
The Largest :: Leatherback Turtle :: The largest turtle, up to about two metres and 700 kilograms, and the only sea turtle without a hard shell — its back is a mosaic of small bones in leathery skin. That, plus its size, lets it keep its body above the water temperature and swim in seas no other turtle can enter.
The Largest :: Chinese Giant Salamander :: The largest amphibian, up to about 1.8 metres, and it is Critically Endangered because it is farmed and eaten. Recent genetics found what was treated as one species is several, which makes the farming worse rather than better: animals of different species have been mixed and released, blurring the wild populations that remain.

! --- The Smallest. Miniature record-holders, and the card says what limits small.
The Smallest :: Bumblebee Bat :: About two grams, and it contests "smallest mammal" with the Etruscan shrew — one is lighter, the other is shorter, which is why both claims are true and neither is settled. It is also the only living species in its genus AND its family, so the smallest mammal is the last of an entire branch.
The Smallest :: Pygmy Marmoset :: The smallest true monkey, about a hundred grams and small enough to sit in a hand. Being small has a cost it pays in diet: it gouges holes in bark with specialised teeth and lives largely on the gum that runs out, because a monkey that size cannot process the bulk foods larger primates use.
The Smallest :: Pygmy Seahorse :: Two centimetres, and it lives on one species of gorgonian coral and matches it exactly — tubercles, colour, everything. Nobody knew it existed until a gorgonian was collected for an aquarium and the coral turned out to have residents. Being small enough not to be found is the whole strategy.
The Smallest :: Least Weasel :: The smallest carnivoran, small enough to follow a vole down its own tunnel — which is the point of the size. The cost is heat: a long thin body loses warmth fast, so it must eat around a third of its body weight a day and cannot afford to stop hunting for long.
The Smallest :: Rusty-spotted Cat :: The smallest wild cat, around one to one and a half kilograms — smaller than most house cats and a fully independent predator. It lives in India and Sri Lanka and is so little studied that most of what is known comes from a handful of camera-trap surveys.
The Smallest :: Pudú :: The smallest deer, standing around thirty-five centimetres at the shoulder in the southern Andes. It is here for the contrast the badge is built on: the same body plan as an elk, at a fraction of the size, because the forest it lives in rewards moving through dense understorey rather than running in the open.
The Smallest :: Volcano Rabbit :: The second-smallest rabbit in the world, restricted to the pine and bunchgrass slopes of four volcanoes near Mexico City — one of the most restricted ranges of any mammal. Unlike most rabbits it has very short ears and it calls in high-pitched notes, which pikas do and rabbits usually do not.
The Smallest :: Kodkod :: The smallest cat in the Americas, about two kilograms, in the temperate forests of Chile and Argentina. Being small and living in one of the most fragmented forests in South America is a bad combination, and it is the badge's reminder that a miniature record-holder usually has a miniature range as well.
The Smallest :: Black-footed Cat :: Africa's smallest cat at about two kilograms, and the deadliest cat on earth by success rate — it makes a kill roughly every fifty minutes and succeeds around sixty percent of the time, where a lion manages about a quarter. Small does not mean less: it hunts all night and walks up to thirty kilometres doing it.
The Smallest :: Dik-dik :: One of the smallest antelopes, about five kilograms, and it never drinks — it gets its water from plants and cools itself by panting through an elongated snout that works as a heat exchanger. It is also monogamous, which is unusual among antelopes and is thought to follow from a territory small enough for one pair to hold.
The Smallest :: Honduran White Bat :: About four centimetres, bright white, and it builds a tent — a group cuts the side veins of a heliconia leaf so it folds down into a shelter they roost under. Sunlight through the green leaf makes the white fur look green from outside, so the smallest builder in the badge is also camouflaged by its own architecture.

! --- The Deepest. Depth records.
The Deepest :: Mariana Snailfish :: The deepest fish ever collected, at about eight kilometres in the Mariana Trench, at roughly eight hundred atmospheres. There appears to be a hard floor for fish at around 8,200 metres, set by the chemistry that stabilises proteins under pressure — so this animal is not just deep, it is near the limit of what a vertebrate can be.
The Deepest :: Cuvier's Beaked Whale :: The deepest dive by any mammal — recorded near three kilometres down, and the longest breath-hold, over three hours. It manages it by collapsing its lungs on the way down, storing oxygen in muscle rather than air, and shutting blood flow away from everything that can wait.
The Deepest :: Southern Elephant Seal :: The deepest-diving seal, past two kilometres, and it does it constantly rather than exceptionally — an adult spends most of the year at sea diving almost continuously, surfacing for a couple of minutes between. It is also the largest carnivoran alive at four tonnes, which is in this badge's favour: the diving is what pays for the size.
The Deepest :: Sperm Whale :: The deepest-diving of the great whales, routinely past a kilometre and recorded far below that, hunting squid in total darkness — which is what the enormous sound-producing organ in its head is for. It is the badge's demonstration that the deep sea is hunted from above as well as inhabited.
The Deepest :: Giant Isopod :: Down to about two kilometres, and it is here for the ecology rather than the record. Depth is not the achievement; surviving on what falls from above is. It is built to gorge on a rare windfall and then wait months or years, which is what living at that depth actually demands.
The Deepest :: Dumbo Octopus :: The deepest-living octopus, found below three kilometres. It flies rather than crawls, using ear-like fins above the eyes, and it has no ink sac at all — a smokescreen is pointless where there is no light to hide in, which is the tidiest illustration in this badge of what depth removes.

! --- The Highest. Altitude records.
The Highest :: Rüppell's Vulture :: The highest-flying bird ever recorded: one struck an aircraft at about eleven thousand metres, which is cruising altitude and roughly the height of Everest plus two kilometres. Its blood carries a haemoglobin variant that grips oxygen unusually tightly, which is what makes air that thin survivable.
The Highest :: Bar-headed Goose :: The highest MIGRATION — it crosses the Himalaya, tracked above seven thousand metres, in a third of the oxygen at sea level, and does the climb in a single push rather than acclimatising over weeks. Denser capillaries in the flight muscle, a heart that pumps more per beat, and a habit of flying at night when the air is cold and dense.
The Highest :: Alpine Chough :: The highest NESTING bird — breeding recorded at around six and a half thousand metres, and birds seen at 8,200 metres on Everest, inside the death zone. It got up there by following people: an expedition is a reliable food source, so the crow of the high mountains learned to work the mountaineers.
The Highest :: Himalayan Jumping Spider :: The highest permanent animal resident known, living in scree at around 6,700 metres on Everest. Nothing grows up there, so there is no food chain for it to sit on top of — it lives entirely on small insects the wind carries up from the valleys and drops.
The Highest :: Snow Leopard :: The highest-living big cat, hunting on ground between three and four and a half thousand metres, and built for the footing rather than the air: enormous paws that work as snowshoes, and a tail nearly as long as its body used as a counterweight on cliffs and as a scarf when it sleeps.
The Highest :: Wild Yak :: The high-altitude bovid, above four thousand metres, with about three times the lung and heart capacity of a domestic cow for its size and blood that carries far more haemoglobin. It also cannot survive at low altitude for long — this is an animal specialised for thin air rather than merely tolerating it.
The Highest :: Andean Condor :: One of the highest-soaring birds, recorded above six thousand metres, and it barely flaps — a study found it spent about one percent of its flight time flapping, and one bird flew over five hours and more than a hundred and seventy kilometres without a single beat. Altitude reached almost entirely on other people's air.
The Highest :: Vicuña :: Lives at three and a half to five thousand metres and has the highest blood haemoglobin concentration of any camelid, with red cells shaped to move through capillaries more easily. It is also the animal the finest natural fibre in the world comes from, which nearly cost it everything before the trade was regulated.
The Highest :: Kiang :: The largest wild ass, on the Tibetan Plateau mostly above four thousand metres, on grass with very little in it and air at around half the oxygen of sea level. It is in this badge as the one with healthy numbers, which on a list of high-altitude specialists is unusual enough to be worth saying.
The Highest :: Gelada :: The highest-living primate, on Ethiopian cliff grassland at up to four and a half thousand metres, and the only primate that lives almost entirely on grass — it spends most of the day sitting and pulling it, which is also why its display moved from the rump to the chest.
The Highest :: Alpine Ibex :: It climbs near-vertical rock, including dam walls, to reach mineral salts, on hooves with a hard rim and a soft grippy centre. It is also a conservation story hiding in an altitude badge: every alpine ibex alive descends from about a hundred animals that survived in one royal reserve at Gran Paradiso.

! --- The Old Ones. Extreme longevity of one INDIVIDUAL, which is the badge's
! --- distinction from an ancient lineage.
The Old Ones :: Greenland Shark :: The longest-lived vertebrate known. Radiocarbon dating of the eye lens — which grows in layers and never renews — put one at around 400 years old, with a large margin, and the method suggests they do not mature until about 150. A shark alive today may have been swimming before the country it is caught by existed.
The Old Ones :: Bowhead Whale :: The longest-lived mammal, over 200 years, and the evidence was accidental: stone and ivory harpoon points from the nineteenth century have been found embedded in living whales. Its genome carries duplicated DNA-repair genes, which is why it is studied by people interested in why large long-lived animals do not get more cancer than they do.
The Old Ones :: Leiopathes Black Coral :: Individual colonies radiocarbon-dated past four thousand years, which makes this among the oldest living animals known — older than the pyramids and still growing. A single pass of a bottom trawl takes out a colony that started before writing did.
The Old Ones :: Isidella Bamboo Coral :: Hundreds to thousands of years old, and it keeps a record of it. The skeleton grows in alternating segments of hard calcite and horny protein, laid down over time, so the bands can be counted and dated like tree rings — a colony is a written account of the ocean it grew in.
The Old Ones :: Giant Barrel Sponge :: Estimated ages past two thousand years, from growth rates measured over decades on Caribbean reefs, which is why it is sometimes called the redwood of the reef. It is also getting commoner as corals decline, so the oldest animal on the reef is one of the few things there currently doing well.
The Old Ones :: Aldabra Giant Tortoise :: The oldest verified living land animal is one of these — Jonathan, on Saint Helena, hatched around 1832 and still alive. The badge's other members are mostly measured by chemistry after death; this one is measured by paperwork, which is a different and much harder kind of evidence to argue with.
The Old Ones :: Pinta Island Tortoise :: Lonesome George was estimated at over a hundred when he died in 2012, and he is here for what the age meant rather than the number. A century-long life makes a species slow to recover and slow to notice losing: by the time anyone realised this one was down to a single animal, it had been down to very few for decades.
The Old Ones :: Sulcata Tortoise :: The badge's ordinary long life, and the one that causes trouble. It lives seventy to a hundred years and grows to around a hundred kilograms, and it is sold as a pet the size of a saucer — so it routinely outlives the household that bought it, and rescue centres in warm countries are full of them.
The Old Ones :: Tuatara :: Over a hundred years, still breeding well past eighty — a male at Southland Museum fathered offspring at about 111. It also has an extraordinarily slow metabolism and a slow molecular clock in some respects, while its DNA has evolved unusually fast in others, which is one of the reasons it gets studied so heavily.
The Old Ones :: Orange Roughy :: Ages past two hundred years, read from growth zones in the ear bones, and it does not breed until around thirty. That is why it is in this badge and why its fishery collapsed: trawlers found the spawning aggregations over seamounts in the 1970s and landed animals older than the boats taking them.

! --- The Fastest. Each one is fastest at a DIFFERENT thing, so the card says
! --- which - and where a famous number is doubtful, it says that too.
The Fastest :: Peregrine Falcon :: The fastest animal there is, in a stoop — measured around 390 kilometres an hour in a dive. Everything about the bird is built for surviving it: baffles in the nostrils to stop the airflow bursting its lungs, a third eyelid wiping the eye clear, and a body it folds into a teardrop. In level flight it is not exceptional.
The Fastest :: Cheetah :: The fastest land animal, around 100 kilometres an hour, and only for about twenty seconds. It runs with a spine that flexes like a spring, semi-retractable claws working as spikes, and a tail used as a rudder for turning at speed — the turning matters more than the top speed, because prey does not run in a straight line.
The Fastest :: Mantis Shrimp :: The fastest strike, not the fastest animal. The club accelerates like a bullet from a rifle over a couple of millimetres, fast enough to boil the water in front of it — and the collapsing cavitation bubble delivers a second blow with a flash of light. It can break aquarium glass.
The Fastest :: Sailfish :: Widely called the fastest fish at over 100 kilometres an hour, and the card has to say plainly that the number is doubtful. It comes from an old, poorly documented measurement, and direct studies of billfish strikes give figures far lower — in the tens of kilometres an hour. What is not in doubt is the acceleration and the bill, which it uses to slash through bait balls.
The Fastest :: Pronghorn :: The fastest animal over DISTANCE — it can hold about 65 kilometres an hour for kilometres, which a cheetah cannot do for a hundred metres. The strange part is that nothing in North America can catch it today: it appears to be running from the American cheetah, a predator that has been extinct for ten thousand years.
The Fastest :: Mexican Free-tailed Bat :: The fastest recorded horizontal flight of any bird or bat — over 160 kilometres an hour in a 2016 study using aircraft tracking, though wind assistance in the measurement is debated. It emerges from Texan caves in columns of millions that show up on weather radar.
The Fastest :: Shortfin Mako :: The fastest shark, with credible figures around 70 to 80 kilometres an hour in bursts, and it leaps clear of the water. It is warm-bodied, keeping its swimming muscle above sea temperature, which is what allows the sustained power — and it is Endangered, largely as bycatch in tuna fisheries.
The Fastest :: Frigatebird :: It holds the record for staying up rather than for going fast: tracked birds have remained airborne for TWO MONTHS without landing, sleeping in flight and climbing to four thousand metres inside cloud to ride the lift. It has the largest wing area for its weight of any bird and cannot land on water — its feathers are not waterproof, so a frigatebird that comes down at sea drowns.
The Fastest :: Golden Eagle :: A stoop measured around 240 to 320 kilometres an hour — second only to the peregrine, and at several times the weight, which makes the forces on it much greater. It hits with the feet rather than the body and can take prey heavier than itself, which is a different problem from the peregrine's.
The Fastest :: Ostrich :: The fastest thing on two legs, about 70 kilometres an hour, and it can hold 50 for half an hour. Its foot is unique among birds: two toes rather than three or four, one of them carrying almost all the weight, which is the same reduction a horse's leg went through for the same reason.

! --- The Slowest. Slow is a strategy, and the card should say what it buys.
The Slowest :: Brown-throated Sloth :: The slowest mammal, and the slowness is a defence: its main predators are the harpy eagle and the jaguar, both of which hunt by detecting movement. It has about half the muscle mass of a mammal its size, digests leaves over weeks, and grows algae in grooved hair — an animal too slow to be seen, carrying its own camouflage as a crop.
The Slowest :: Greenland Shark :: It cruises at about one kilometre an hour, slower than a person walking, and still eats seals — which is a genuine puzzle. The leading explanation is that it takes them while they sleep in the water. Everything about it is slow: it grows about a centimetre a year and does not mature for around 150 of them.
The Slowest :: Sulcata Tortoise :: Slow in the ordinary way, and here for what that buys: an animal that walks at a few hundred metres an hour survives by carrying its shelter and by digging. In the Sahel it excavates burrows several metres long to escape heat, so the slowest thing on the plain is also the one that made the hole everything else uses.
The Slowest :: Olm :: The slowest life in the badge. It can go a decade without eating, moves almost not at all in the caves it lives in, and has been recorded staying in the same spot for over seven years. It lives around a century and reproduces roughly once every dozen years. Slowness here is a response to a habitat with almost no food in it.
The Slowest :: Slow Loris :: It moves hand over hand with no sudden motion at all, which lets it approach roosting birds and sleeping insects without registering as an approach. It also has extra blood vessels in its limbs that let it grip a branch for hours without the muscles tiring — slowness supported by anatomy rather than just by patience.
The Slowest :: Pygmy Seahorse :: Seahorses are among the slowest fish in the sea, propelled by a small dorsal fin beating rapidly for very little forward motion, and this one barely travels at all — it lives on a single gorgonian and matches it. Speed is irrelevant when the strategy is to not be found in the first place.
The Slowest :: Japanese Sea Lily :: An animal that looks like a plant and spent most of its evolutionary history not moving. It filters what the current brings, which requires no speed — and the detail that earns it this badge is that it CAN move: it detaches and crawls away on its arms to re-anchor, at a pace that had to be filmed to be believed.
The Slowest :: Giant Green Anemone :: Fixed to a rock, and not permanently — anemones can and do relocate, at centimetres per hour. Its slowness is what makes the partnership in Strange Allies possible: an animal that stays put for years is a reliable address for the algae in its tissue and for whatever shelters in its tentacles.
The Slowest :: Partula nodosa :: A tree snail, and the badge's smallest slow animal — which is exactly why it could be wiped out. It cannot outrun the introduced rosy wolfsnail that hunts it, and it cannot disperse to a valley the predator has not reached. Slowness is only a strategy while nothing arrives that is faster.
The Slowest :: Pinta Island Tortoise :: Slow to move, slow to mature, slow to breed — the pattern that makes a giant tortoise easy to remove and almost impossible to restore. Whalers took them by the thousand because they could be stacked alive in a hold for a year, which is a use only a slow, long-lived, uncomplaining animal could be put to.
The Slowest :: Aldabra Giant Tortoise :: The slow animal that shapes its own island: grazing at a walking pace maintains a low community of plants called tortoise turf that exists because the tortoises are there. Over a century and a half of life, one grazer reworks the same ground repeatedly — slow, and cumulative, which is the badge's real argument.
