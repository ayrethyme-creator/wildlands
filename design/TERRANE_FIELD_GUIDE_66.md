# Terrane field-guide entries: the approved sixty-six

This is the reviewable writing source for the sixty-six approved animal pictures
identified after the second Terrane art audit. It is **not** the generated Safari
Saga export in `design/FIELD_GUIDE.txt`, and that file must not be edited by hand.

The batch contains twenty-one animals for The Floe, fifteen for The Divide,
fourteen domesticated forms for The Breeding Centre, six companion animals for
The Kept, five recently extinct animals for The Vigil, and five animals finishing
smaller biome batches. Eight rejected pictures and the deliberately deferred
fossil and deep-sea sets are outside this writing batch.

The familiar name leads. The italic line identifies the exact species carrying
the claims. Where the familiar name is a breed or morph rather than a species,
the line says so and identifies its parent species. Counts are dated research
values, not timeless constants. These entries still need ordinary claim-by-claim
review before they can be marked VERIFIED in `design/CLAIMS.txt`.

## The Floe: polar

### Adélie Penguin

*Pygoscelis adeliae* · one of 3 living species currently placed in *Pygoscelis*

Adélies are the brisk, quarrelsome pebble-builders of the Antarctic coast. A
pair raises its nest just high enough to keep meltwater from soaking the egg,
and a particularly fine stone may be courted, guarded, or stolen from next
door. Once breeding is over, these penguins follow the pack ice rather than
remaining at the colony. They hunt fish and krill from the water, then may save
energy on land by lying on their bellies and pushing themselves over the snow.
Their formal black-and-white dress is real; so is the muddy, noisy neighborhood
hidden behind it.

### Antarctic Icefish

*Pagetodes antarcticus* · also called the Long-fingered Icefish, one of 3 living *Pagetodes* species

Like every white-blooded icefish, this species carries one of the Floe’s
strangest adaptations: its adult blood contains no haemoglobin, so oxygen
travels dissolved in plasma rather than bound to red pigment. Cold,
oxygen-rich water makes that possible, helped by the enlarged circulation
shared across the family. It lives roughly 90 to 600 metres deep around the
Antarctic continental shelf, feeding on fish and krill; its larvae spend a
long late-winter phase in open water. Older guides call it *Cryodraco
antarcticus*, while current fish lists place it in *Pagetodes*.

### Antarctic Sea Spider

*Colossendeis megalonyx* · one of 74 accepted species currently listed in *Colossendeis* by WoRMS, but probably a complex of several lineages

An Antarctic sea spider is neither a true spider nor built like most animals
you know. Its body is so slight that much of its gut extends into its long
legs, and oxygen passes across the outer surface instead of through gills or
lungs. *Colossendeis megalonyx* prowls the seafloor and can seize surprisingly
mobile prey, including the swimming sea angel *Clione antarctica*. Yet the
familiar name may hide more than one species: genetic work has uncovered
several distinct, sometimes hybridising lineages beneath the single label. On
the Floe, meeting one is also meeting a taxonomic mystery still in motion.

### Antarctic Toothfish

*Dissostichus mawsoni* · one of 2 living species currently placed in *Dissostichus*

The Antarctic toothfish is a patient giant of cold, deep water. Antifreeze
glycoproteins keep ice crystals from growing in its body fluids, while stored
lipids and lightly mineralised bones help it hover without the gas-filled swim
bladder used by many fishes. That near-neutral buoyancy lets a heavy predator
move through the water column for fish and squid with little wasted effort. It
grows slowly, matures late, and produces relatively few young, so a lost adult
is not quickly replaced. Beneath the plain, dark silhouette is a carefully
balanced solution to life below freezing.

### Arctic Char

*Salvelinus alpinus* · one of 51 species currently listed in *Salvelinus* by FishBase; species boundaries within the char complex remain difficult

Arctic char refuse to live only one kind of life. Some remain in a lake from
hatching to old age; others travel to the sea each summer, feed hard for a few
short weeks, then return to fresh water before winter. Sea-run char must make
that return because the coldest marine water can become lethal, while lakes and
rivers offer a safer refuge beneath the ice. Isolated waters can also produce
strikingly different sizes, colours, diets, and breeding forms. Scientists
still debate where variation ends and separate char species begin, making
*Salvelinus alpinus* both a survivor and a living lesson in evolution.

### Arctic Cod

*Boreogadus saida* · the only living species currently placed in *Boreogadus*

Arctic cod are small enough to disappear beneath the ice, yet much of the
Arctic food web depends on them. Seabirds, seals, whales, and larger fishes all
turn their stored energy into lives of their own. The cod can remain active in
seawater below the usual freezing point of fish blood because antifreeze
glycoproteins block dangerous ice growth. Remarkably, that molecular trick
evolved independently from the similar protection found in Antarctic fishes.
Young cod often shelter and feed around sea ice, making this unassuming silver
fish a moving bridge between plankton below and predators above.

### Arctic Tern

*Sterna paradisaea* · one of 13 living species currently placed in *Sterna* by AviList

Arctic terns stitch the planet’s polar summers together. Tiny tracking devices
have recorded individuals covering more than 80,000 kilometres in a year,
leaving northern breeding grounds for productive waters near Antarctic pack ice
and returning by a different, wind-assisted route. They do not simply fly
straight south: broad loops help them find food and favourable air currents
over several oceans. At the nest, the same elegant bird becomes a fierce
defender, diving at intruders with its sharp bill. Its journey is not one
heroic dash but thousands of precise choices about wind, water, and season.

### Arctic Wolf

*Canis lupus arctos* · a widely used subspecific name within the gray wolf; *Canis* currently contains 8 living species in the Mammal Diversity Database

The Arctic wolf is a gray wolf shaped by open tundra, long darkness, and prey
scattered over enormous distances. On Ellesmere Island, one GPS-collared male
and its pack ranged across roughly 6,640 square kilometres, hunting muskoxen,
Peary caribou, and Arctic hares. Pale fur helps against a snowy background, but
cooperation matters more than colour: a pack can search widely, test large
prey, and share an unpredictable landscape. The name *arctos* is commonly used
for the high-Arctic form, although subspecies boundaries in gray wolves depend
on the authority and remain less settled than the species itself.

### Capelin

*Mallotus villosus* · Atlantic Capelin, one of 2 accepted named *Mallotus* species; a third Atlantic lineage remains unnamed

Capelin turn an ordinary beach into a silver tide. In summer, some populations
surge into shallow water and roll onto sand or fine gravel to spawn, often at
night and near high tide. The eggs stick to the substrate instead of drifting
away, while many exhausted adults, especially males, die after the event.
Offshore, this little smelt is a keystone forage fish: cod, seabirds, seals,
and whales all follow its abundance. A capelin run therefore feeds two worlds
at once, planting the next generation in the shore and carrying ocean energy
into a crowd of waiting hunters.

### Collared Lemming

*Dicrostonyx groenlandicus* · one of 7 living species currently recognised in *Dicrostonyx* by the Mammal Diversity Database; some population boundaries remain unresolved

The collared lemming performs a winter transformation no other rodent genus
completes in quite the same way. Shortening days trigger brown summer fur to
become white, while the front claws broaden into paired, shovel-like tips for
digging through hard snow. Beneath that cover it searches for stems and buds,
hidden from wind and many predators. Not every island population changes
equally, a clue that local winters have shaped the response. Even its map is
unfinished: the current species treatment tentatively absorbs at least one
named form that still lacks decisive molecular study.

### Gentoo Penguin

*Pygoscelis papua* · treated by AviList v2025b as one of 3 living *Pygoscelis* species, while recent studies propose splitting gentoo lineages into 4 species

Gentoos approach Antarctic family life with unusual flexibility. A pair builds
a low nest from pebbles, grass, or whatever the colony offers, then guards the
small territory around it with great seriousness. Two eggs are usual, and older
chicks gather into crèches while both parents forage. Breeding dates and diets
vary markedly from one island group to another, matching local seasons rather
than one rigid calendar. Those differences run deeper than behaviour: genetic
and anatomical studies, including a 2026 revision, argue that “gentoo” contains
four species. The latest released global checklist has not yet adopted that
split.

### Greenland Halibut

*Reinhardtius hippoglossoides* · the only living species currently placed in *Reinhardtius*

Greenland halibut begin life upright and symmetrical. As a larva settles, one
eye travels toward the upper side, the skull twists, and the young fish becomes
a dark, diamond-shaped flatfish. Its migrating eye does not move quite as far
as in many relatives, leaving unusually broad vision around the body. Nor is
this halibut glued to the seabed: tagged fish make daily vertical journeys and
may spend about a quarter of their time in the water column. In cold northern
depths, it is both bottom-dweller and roaming hunter, built to watch more than
one direction.

### Ivory Gull

*Pagophila eburnea* · the only living species currently placed in *Pagophila*

An adult ivory gull is almost entirely white, a bright bird against blue ice
and dark leads. It remains tied to the Arctic ice through every season, feeding
along cracks and polynyas where fish and invertebrates approach the surface. It
is also an expert scavenger, arriving at seal remains left by polar bears or
hunters and turning scraps into flight. Satellite-tagged Canadian birds spent
about 90 percent of winter over sea ice, including long periods above dense
pack. That dependence makes the gull a beautiful specialist, but also leaves it
with few substitutes when ice conditions change.

### King Penguin

*Aptenodytes patagonicus* · one of 2 living species currently placed in *Aptenodytes*

King penguins raise a chick on a calendar longer than an Antarctic year.
Courtship, a single egg, incubation, winter fasting, and chick growth can
occupy 13 to 16 months, so successful parents often cannot repeat the feat the
next season. There is no nest: the adult balances the egg on its feet beneath a
warm fold of skin, trading duty with its partner. Chicks later stand together
in dense groups while adults travel for lanternfish and squid. A king colony
looks crowded and chaotic, but every returning bird must find one partner or
chick by voice.

### Little Auk

*Alle alle* · the only living species currently placed in *Alle*

The little auk is a pocket-sized seabird with an ecosystem-sized effect. It
gathers fat-rich copepods from cold Arctic water in a throat pouch, then
carries them to a single chick hidden among scree and rock crevices. Millions
breed in noisy colonies, and their guano moves marine nutrients uphill into
tundra plants, insects, and streams. At one Svalbard colony of 50,000 pairs,
researchers estimated roughly 100 tonnes of droppings reached the surrounding
land in a breeding season. A warmer ocean can replace large Arctic copepods
with smaller prey, forcing this tiny courier to work harder for every meal.

### Long-tailed Duck

*Clangula hyemalis* · the only living species currently placed in *Clangula*

The long-tailed duck seems determined never to wear the same outfit for long.
Males reverse much of their dark-and-light pattern between summer and winter,
with overlapping moults creating more seasonal plumages than most ducks. The
narrow central tail feathers remain a flourish, but the real performance
happens underwater. These sea ducks propel themselves with their wings and have
been recorded foraging near 60 metres deep; during a feeding bout they can
spend three or four times as long submerged as at the surface. They breed
beside tundra pools, then gather on coasts where their yodelling calls travel
over winter water.

### Ringed Seal

*Pusa hispida* · one of 4 living species currently recognised in *Pusa* by the Mammal Diversity Database

Ringed seals manufacture openings in a world trying to freeze shut. Thick
foreflipper claws scrape and maintain breathing holes through ice that can
exceed 1.8 metres, and drifting snow above a hole becomes material for a hidden
lair. A mother gives birth and nurses inside that snow cave, where the pup
gains shelter from cold and from watchful predators. Polar bears, however,
learn to search the same ice for those chambers, making each lair both nursery
and risk. Recent taxonomy raised the isolated Saimaa seal from a ringed-seal
subspecies to a separate species, bringing the current *Pusa* count to four.

### Rock Ptarmigan

*Lagopus muta* · one of 3 living species currently placed in *Lagopus*

Rock ptarmigan change with the ground beneath them. Mottled brown feathers
blend into summer tundra, while a mostly white winter coat erases the bird
against snow; males retain a crisp black eye-stripe that can betray them at
close range. Their legs and toes are densely feathered for warmth and support,
and long claws can excavate a snow burrow within seconds. Inside, the air is
calmer and warmer than the exposed ridge, allowing a bird to wait out wind for
hours or even days. What looks like disappearance is an active routine of
moulting, digging, and choosing shelter.

### Ross's Gull

*Rhodostethia rosea* · the only living species currently placed in *Rhodostethia*

Ross’s gull seems to migrate the wrong way at first: after nesting on tundra
wetlands, many birds head north into broken sea ice in the central Arctic
Ocean. A small satellite study followed two Siberian breeders; one travelled at
least 8,254 kilometres, lingering between 80 and 85 degrees north before
crossing toward Alaska and Chukotka. Canadian birds may instead winter in the
Northwest Atlantic, so there is no single route for the whole species. In
breeding dress, a soft pink wash and fine black collar give this slight gull an
almost delicate look, concealing a life spent over remote, restless water.

### Snow Petrel

*Pagodroma nivea* · the sole living species currently placed in *Pagodroma*; 2 size-based subspecies are widely recognised

Snow petrels carry Antarctic whiteness into the air: white wings and body,
black eyes and bill, all skimming above pack ice. They lay one egg in a
protected rock crevice, sometimes far beyond any ordinary idea of a seabird
colony. In the Prince Charles Mountains, nests occur as far as about 440
kilometres inland, requiring immense commutes to marine feeding grounds. Two
size forms, usually treated as subspecies, overlap and can form mixed pairs, so
their exact taxonomic meaning remains debated. Even fossilised nest deposits
preserve a long record of where ice-free rock and reachable ocean once met.

### South Polar Skua

*Stercorarius maccormicki* · one of 7 living species currently placed in *Stercorarius* by AviList

South polar skuas are adaptable hunters with two very different Antarctic
lives. Near penguin colonies, some pairs take eggs and unattended chicks;
elsewhere, birds depend chiefly on fish and krill brought from the sea. They
nest in a shallow scrape on exposed ground and defend it with fearless, low
attacks. When the brief breeding season ends, the map opens dramatically.
Tracking and sightings show individuals ranging into the North Atlantic and
North Pacific, crossing much of the planet before returning south. The older
genus name *Catharacta* still appears in some Antarctic sources, but current
AviList places all seven skuas and jaegers in *Stercorarius*.

## The Divide: mountain

### Alpine Chamois

*Rupicapra rupicapra* · one of 2 living *Rupicapra* species

One of two living *Rupicapra* chamois, it crosses broken Alpine ground on hooves
whose hard rims bite into rock while softer centres grip uneven surfaces. Winter
is met partly by turning the body down: field studies recorded lower heart rate,
body temperature and activity as food became scarce. Not every chamois follows
the same seasonal route, either. Some descend or shelter in forest while others
remain high, making this less a simple tale of an animal that always climbs than
one that balances snow, forage, disturbance and the energy left in its body.

### Alpine Chough

*Pyrrhocorax graculus* · one of 2 living *Pyrrhocorax* choughs

One of two living *Pyrrhocorax* choughs, it keeps to high mountains with red
legs, a curved yellow bill and a talent for finding food in company. Flocks feed
together through winter rather than abandoning the heights altogether. Ski
stations and mountain restaurants have added a new kind of patch to that map:
recent tracking shows that predictable human food can reshape where these birds
spend their day. The chough circling a summit café is therefore not merely a
comic thief. It is a social alpine forager rapidly learning how a human season
has rearranged its mountain.

### Alpine Salamander

*Salamandra atra* · one of 6 living *Salamandra* species

One of six living *Salamandra* salamanders, this glossy black mountaineer can
complete reproduction without a pond. Instead of laying eggs into water, a
female carries developing young through a prolonged pregnancy and usually gives
birth on land to two fully formed salamanders. Embryos first consume their own
yolk and then receive further nourishment within the mother's oviduct. That
slow, costly route bypasses the free-swimming larval stage that defines many
amphibian lives, turning a damp crevice in an Alpine slope into habitat for an
animal whose young arrive ready for land.

### Apollo Butterfly

*Parnassius apollo* · one of about 50 *Parnassius* species; exact limits remain disputed

One of about fifty *Parnassius* butterflies, the Apollo carries red eyespots
across pale wings whose sparse scales can make their edges seem translucent.
Its caterpillars depend chiefly on stonecrops growing in warm, open and rocky
places. That makes an old grazing landscape unexpectedly important: when
pastures are abandoned, shrubs and trees can close the sunny gaps on which the
larvae rely. More than two hundred Apollo subspecies have been proposed, many on
uncertain boundaries, so both its name-map and its habitat-map are works in
progress. Protecting it often means preserving openness rather than leaving a
slope entirely untouched.

### Argali

*Ovis ammon* · one of 7 living *Ovis* sheep in the Mammal Diversity Database

One of seven living *Ovis* sheep in the current Mammal Diversity Database, the
argali is the largest wild sheep. Mature rams carry immense corkscrewing horns
and meet rivals in head-on clashes whose sound carries across open mountain
country. Those horns are spectacular but expensive: growing and carrying them
demands resources in a landscape where forage is seasonal and thin. Argali
range across separated highlands in Central Asia, and the named regional forms
do not all have equally settled boundaries. The familiar silhouette therefore
covers a wide and varied species, not one uniform mountain population.

### Bar-headed Goose

*Anser indicus* · one of 11 living *Anser* geese in AviList

One of eleven living *Anser* geese in AviList, it migrates across the Himalaya
where cold air carries only a fraction of the oxygen available at sea level.
Its solution is not one miraculous organ: high-affinity haemoglobin and changes
through the lungs, circulation and flight muscles help the whole oxygen pathway
work at altitude. Tracking also corrected the heroic picture of a single flat
flight above every summit. Birds often follow a roller-coaster route, losing
height after crossings and climbing steeply when the terrain demands it. The
journey remains extraordinary precisely because it is an exercise in careful
energy use, not effortless flight.

### Himalayan Jumping Spider

*Euophrys omnisuperstes* · one of 100 accepted *Euophrys* species in the World Spider Catalog

One of one hundred accepted *Euophrys* jumping spiders, this tiny hunter was
confirmed from adults collected near 5,900 metres on Everest. Immature spiders
probably belonging to the same species were found still higher, at about 6,700
metres, but could not be identified with equal certainty. It does not spin a
web to catch prey. Instead it stalks and pounces on minute springtails and flies
carried uphill by the wind. Its name means roughly “standing above all”: an apt
label for a predator supported by an aerial trickle of life far above the last
plants.

### Kiang

*Equus kiang* · one of 9 living *Equus* species in the Mammal Diversity Database, including 2 domestic forms

One of nine living *Equus* species in the current Mammal Diversity Database,
the kiang is the largest wild ass. It lives on the Tibetan Plateau in loose,
shifting groups, travelling between grassland, steppe and water as local
conditions change. Those movements are real but do not fit one universal
long-distance migration: studies and regional accounts describe flexible
seasonal responses rather than a single route followed by every population.
Its chestnut summer coat grows dark and woolly for winter, while a white belly
and muzzle remain sharply marked against the high, open ground.

### Lammergeier

*Gypaetus barbatus* · the only living species in *Gypaetus*

The only living species in *Gypaetus* is a vulture built around what other
scavengers leave behind. It swallows smaller bones and carries larger ones into
the air, dropping them on favoured rocky “ossuaries” until useful pieces break
free. Bone is not empty packaging: marrow and fat make it a concentrated food
that can remain available after softer flesh has gone. Adults also seek
iron-rich mud and water that stain their naturally pale feathers rusty orange.
The colour is applied, not grown, so the famous mountain beard and ochre breast
are partly the bird's own work.

### Mountain Goat

*Oreamnos americanus* · the only living species in *Oreamnos*

The only living species in *Oreamnos* is called a goat but belongs to the
goat-antelope branch rather than the genus of true goats. Its two hoof toes can
spread and move independently; hard outer edges catch tiny ledges while softer
pads mould against stone. Powerful shoulders then haul the body upward, making
steep cliffs both feeding ground and refuge from predators. Kids enter that
vertical world quickly, following their mothers over terrain where a misplaced
step matters. What looks like impossible balance is a combination of foot
structure, strength and a lifetime of choosing footholds.

### Rock Hyrax

*Procavia capensis* · the only living species in *Procavia*

The only living species in *Procavia* resembles a blunt, tailless rabbit, but
its deeper family tree lies with elephants and sea cows in the Afrotherian
branch of mammals. Broad, rubbery foot pads help it cling to rock; moisture from
specialised glands increases their grip. Its temperature control is less exact
than that of many similarly sized mammals, so a colony makes the landscape do
part of the work, crowding into shelter at night and basking together after
sunrise. The pile of warm bodies on a boulder is therefore not idleness but
social thermoregulation.

### Tibetan Antelope

*Pantholops hodgsonii* · the only living species in *Pantholops*

The only living species in *Pantholops*, the Tibetan antelope or chiru carries
an undercoat so fine that it is woven into shahtoosh. The fibre cannot be
harvested like sheep's wool: obtaining it has meant killing and skinning the
animal, which drove heavy poaching. Females also make long seasonal journeys to
traditional calving grounds, sometimes moving towards land with poorer forage
before returning with young. That apparent puzzle suggests that safety,
tradition or other benefits matter more than the richest immediate meal. A soft
shawl and a distant nursery have both shaped this high-plateau species' fate.

### Wallcreeper

*Tichodroma muraria* · the only living species in *Tichodroma* and Tichodromidae

The only living species in both *Tichodroma* and its family moves over sheer
cliffs like a bird-sized moth. Grey plumage disappears against stone until the
wings open and reveal crimson panels dotted with white. It hops, flutters and
probes into cracks for insects and spiders, using rock faces as another bird
might use tree bark. In winter many descend to lower cliffs, quarries and old
walls. The name describes the performance exactly: a wall-creeper turns bare
vertical ground into a searchable surface, flashing colour only when it shifts
to the next patch.

### White-rumped Vulture

*Gyps bengalensis* · one of 8 living *Gyps* vultures in AviList

One of eight living *Gyps* vultures, it shows how a medicine can pass through a
food web. Veterinary diclofenac relieved pain in cattle but residues in their
carcasses caused fatal kidney failure in vultures arriving to clean them up.
South Asian populations fell by more than 99 percent in little more than a
decade. Restrictions on the drug and conservation breeding have slowed parts
of the decline, but recovery begins from a tiny remnant. A scavenger once so
common that it was almost background became Critically Endangered because the
last meal had been treated before the animal died.

### Woolly Flying Squirrel

*Eupetaurus cinereus* · Western Woolly Flying Squirrel, one of 3 living *Eupetaurus* species

One of three living *Eupetaurus* woolly flying squirrels, this is the western
species left after a 2021 revision separated Himalayan and Tibetan populations.
It is among the largest gliding mammals, stretching a skin membrane between its
limbs to cross darkness rather than using powered flight. Its high-crowned
teeth suit an abrasive diet, and analysed droppings consisted overwhelmingly of
pine needles—an austere food for such a large animal. Long known from very few
specimens, it shows how a familiar name can hide several mountain lineages until
museum material, field evidence and DNA are considered together.

## The Long Grass: savanna

### Gemsbok

*Oryx gazella* · one of 4 living *Oryx* species

One of four living *Oryx* antelopes, the gemsbok can range far from surface
water in the Kalahari; biologged animals were often more than twenty kilometres
from a possible source. In hot seasons they move more activity into the night
and choose shade during the day, conserving water through behaviour as well as
physiology. Both females and males carry long, nearly straight horns, so the
armed silhouette does not identify the sex. The animal's desert reputation is
not a claim that it never drinks, but a capacity to keep functioning when the
next drink is distant and uncertain.

## The Weald: forest

### Iberian Lynx

*Lynx pardinus* · one of 4 living *Lynx* species

One of four living *Lynx* cats, the Iberian lynx is unusually dependent on one
prey: the European rabbit. When rabbit disease and habitat loss struck, only 62
mature lynx were estimated in 2001. Protected scrubland, restored prey,
breeding, translocation and reintroduction helped the total population pass
2,000, and the IUCN moved the species from Endangered to Vulnerable in 2024.
That is a remarkable recovery, not a finished rescue. Rabbit outbreaks, roads,
disease and low genetic diversity still matter, making this a case where
success means continuing the work that made success possible.

### Wood Frog

*Lithobates sylvaticus* · one of 45 living *Lithobates* species in ASW; also treated as *Rana sylvatica*

One of forty-five *Lithobates* frogs in Amphibian Species of the World, it can
survive winter with much of its body water frozen outside the cells. Ice on the
skin triggers the liver to release a flood of glucose, while urea accumulated
before winter adds protection; both limit cellular damage as circulation and
breathing stop. Northern Alaskan frogs have survived laboratory freezing to
minus 16 degrees Celsius, a capacity stronger than in many southern
populations. Some taxonomies return it to *Rana*, so the name is unsettled even
though the frozen frog's return to activity after thawing is well demonstrated.

## The Dry: desert

### Mexican Redknee

*Brachypelma hamorii* · one of 8 accepted *Brachypelma* species in the World Spider Catalog

One of eight accepted *Brachypelma* tarantulas, this orange-kneed burrower has
often reached captivity under the wrong name. A 2017 revision showed that many
spiders long called *B. smithi* were actually *B. hamorii*, while retaining a
separate true *B. smithi*. When threatened, it can rake barbed urticating hairs
from its abdomen into the air instead of relying on a bite; the hairs irritate
skin and eyes and leave a temporary bald patch. International trade protection
covers the genus, so a famous pet also carries a lesson in identification:
conservation counts depend on knowing which animal was moved.

## The Strand: coast

### Little Blue Penguin

*Eudyptula minor* · the only living *Eudyptula* in AviList; Australian birds are sometimes split

The only living *Eudyptula* species in AviList is also the smallest living
penguin, standing roughly thirty centimetres tall. It hunts small fish and squid
at sea by day, then usually returns after dusk to a burrow, crevice or sheltered
nest on land. Its upper feathers are slate blue rather than the black worn by
most penguins, giving the familiar Little Blue name. Genetic differences
between Australian and New Zealand lineages have led some authorities to split
the Australian Little Penguin as *Eudyptula novaehollandiae*; AviList currently
keeps those birds within this one species.

## The Breeding Centre: domestic forms

### Anatolian Shepherd

*Canis familiaris* · a domestic breed of *Canis lupus*; one of 8 living *Canis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

This is a livestock guardian, not a separate species and not quite the same
kind of worker as a herding dog. Anatolian Shepherds were selected to stay with
flocks, notice trouble and make decisions at a distance from a handler. Size,
speed and a calm willingness to stand their ground can deter a predator before
a fight begins. That independence was useful on open country; in a household it
also means that early socialisation, secure boundaries and thoughtful handling
matter. Beneath the specialised working build is the same domestic animal as
every village dog and lapdog: a human-shaped form of the gray wolf.

### Angora Rabbit

*Oryctolagus cuniculus* · a domestic wool-breed group; one of 2 living *Oryctolagus* species

“Angora rabbit” is not one wild species, and even among breeders it is not one
universal breed. The American Rabbit Breeders Association recognises English,
French, Giant and Satin Angoras separately, all descended from the European
rabbit. Their unusually long, fine fibre is gathered by combing, plucking loose
wool or shearing, depending on breed and husbandry. The cloudlike coat is a
human-selected production trait with a cost: without regular grooming it can
mat, and swallowed fibre can accumulate when gut movement falters. What looks
like ornament is therefore living husbandry — generations of selection that
must be matched by generations of care.

### Ball Python Morph

*Python regius* · a captive-bred colour or pattern form, not a species; one of 10 living *Python* species

A morph is a heritable version of a ball python's colour or pattern, not a new
kind of snake. Breeders have preserved rare variants and combined them into
“designer” animals; research using harmless samples of shed skin has begun to
identify the machinery beneath the names. Albino, Lavender Albino and Ultramel
morphs are associated with variants in *TYR*, *OCA2* and *TYRP1*, while a
studied Piebald line carries a disruptive *tfec* variant. The palette may be
extraordinary, but every one remains *Python regius*, with the same species-
level needs and the same defensive habit of coiling around its head that gave
the ball python its familiar name.

### Bred Axolotl

*Ambystoma mexicanum* · a captive-bred form of one of 26 living *Ambystoma* species

Captive breeding has produced white, golden, dark and mottled axolotls, but the
colours do not make separate species. An axolotl normally keeps larval features
through adulthood: feathery external gills remain, and the animal breeds while
still fully aquatic. It can regrow a lost limb and repair several other tissues
without the heavy scarring familiar in mammals, which is why laboratory lines
have become so valuable. That abundance can conceal the other half of its
story. Wild *A. mexicanum* survives only in the altered waterways of Xochimilco
and is Critically Endangered; a tank full of descendants is not a restored
lake, nor a substitute for its wild population.

### Bull Terrier

*Canis familiaris* · a domestic breed of *Canis lupus*; one of 8 living *Canis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

The Bull Terrier's long, convex “egg-shaped” head and small triangular eyes are
not the marks of another canine species. They are an extreme, recognisable
result of people choosing which domestic dogs reproduced. The breed's muscular
body preserves its bull-and-terrier history, while generations of selection for
companionship produced the exuberant modern house dog familiar to owners. A
silhouette can tell a history of breeding, but it cannot finish the biography
of the animal inside it: health, early experience, training and individual
temperament still matter. The honest field-guide label is therefore breed,
never species — one branch of domestic dog diversity shaped by a written
standard.

### Burmese

*Felis catus* · a domestic breed derived from *Felis lybica*; one of 7 living *Felis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

The modern American Burmese breeding programme began with Wong Mau, a small
brown cat brought from Burma to the United States in 1930. Planned matings,
including early crosses with Siamese cats, showed that her warm “sepia” colour
could breed true and established a new registry population. Today's compact,
heavy-feeling Burmese comes in several accepted colours, depending on the
registry; “Burmese” and “European Burmese” are also judged as separate breeds
in some systems. Those boundaries are human pedigree rules, not reproductive
barriers in nature. The animal is still a domestic cat — the same parent
species beneath a carefully maintained coat, body shape and family record.

### Cornish Rex

*Felis catus* · a domestic breed derived from *Felis lybica*; one of 7 living *Felis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

In Cornwall in 1950, an otherwise ordinary farm cat produced a curly-coated
kitten named Kallibunker. Breeding showed that his unusual coat was inherited
as a recessive trait, and that chance mutation became the foundation of the
Cornish Rex. Its very soft waves are made mostly from fine undercoat rather
than the usual layered coat with prominent guard hairs; even the whiskers may
curl. Warm skin and a light coat can make the cat feel unusually hot to a hand,
but it does not run at a different feline temperature. A single inherited
variant changed the texture people see, not the species of the cat beneath it.

### Domestic Fox

*Vulpes vulpes* · a behaviourally selected captive red-fox line, not a separate species; one of 12 living *Vulpes* species

The famous “domesticated fox” experiment did not begin by lifting untouched
wild foxes into a laboratory. In 1959, researchers founded selection lines from
red foxes already bred on fur farms, then repeatedly chose animals by their
response to people. Over generations the tame line approached willingly,
sought contact and showed less fear; changes in development, coat and body form
also appeared. The result is powerful evidence that selection on behaviour can
reshape an animal, but it is not a new species and not proof that any rescued
wild fox makes an easy pet. It is a particular experimental population of the
red fox, with a documented breeding history.

### Egyptian Mau

*Felis catus* · a domestic breed derived from *Felis lybica*; one of 7 living *Felis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

The Egyptian Mau is a registry breed whose standard asks for distinct spots,
including markings that need not follow the orderly rosettes of a wild cat. A
loose fold of skin between flank and hind leg and slightly longer hind limbs
help give it a long-striding outline. “Mau” and the spotted coat invite an easy
story of an unbroken line from cats painted in ancient Egypt, but modern breed
history and an evocative name cannot prove that pedigree across millennia. The
solid claim is more interesting than the romance: selective breeding stabilised
a rare domestic-cat pattern, and people agreed on the features by which that
population would be recognised.

### Fancy Pigeon

*Columba livia* · a domestic fancy form of the Rock Pigeon; one of 32 living *Columba* species

Pouters, tumblers, fantails and many other fancy pigeons are variations of one
parent species, the Rock Pigeon. Fanciers amplified small inherited differences
into crests, feathered feet, inflated crops, unusual flight and tails spread
like fans; Darwin used that astonishing range to explain what selection can do.
The bird under every costume keeps a distinctly pigeon form of parenthood. Both
sexes make “crop milk,” a nutrient-rich shedding from the crop lining, and feed
it to their squabs. City pigeons, racing birds and exhibition champions can
look worlds apart, yet they remain members of the same freely interbreeding
domestic lineage.

### French Bulldog

*Canis familiaris* · a domestic breed of *Canis lupus*; one of 8 living *Canis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

Bat ears and a short, square muzzle make the French Bulldog readable from a
distance. Both are breed traits chosen by people, not signs of a distinct
species. The shortened skull is also more than a look: in some dogs, crowded
soft tissues and narrowed airways cause brachycephalic obstructive airway
syndrome, making breathing, exercise and cooling harder. Severity varies, so
the fair response is neither to romanticise the snort nor to diagnose an animal
from its photograph. Breeding away from exaggeration, health screening and
veterinary assessment can protect the companion behind the silhouette. A
fashionable outline carries a real human responsibility.

### Manx

*Felis catus* · a domestic breed derived from *Felis lybica*; one of 7 living *Felis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

Not every Manx is completely tailless. Breeders describe a continuum from
“rumpy” cats with no visible tail through risers and stumpies to fully tailed
“longies.” The inherited Manx variant is incompletely dominant; embryos with
two copies generally do not survive, and one copy can alter the final vertebrae
by very different amounts. In some kittens the same developmental change also
brings spinal, bowel or bladder problems, so a missing tail must never be
treated as harmless decoration by default. Tailed cats remain part of Manx
breeding programmes. The breed is one domestic-cat population organised around
a variable mutation, not a naturally tailless species.

### Munchkin

*Felis catus* · a short-legged domestic form derived from *Felis lybica*; one of 7 living *Felis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

The Munchkin's short limbs come from a dominant inherited change affecting long-
bone growth. Its body, head and coat can otherwise take many ordinary domestic-
cat forms because early programmes deliberately outcrossed short-legged cats to
the wider cat population. Registry status is not universal: TICA recognises the
breed, while other major registries do not, and welfare debate continues around
selecting a skeletal difference for appearance. That uncertainty belongs in
the guide. “Munchkin” identifies a human-maintained trait and pedigree system,
not a new species, and the charm of a low stance does not remove the obligation
to follow mobility and health evidence as it develops.

### Silkworm

*Bombyx mori* · a domesticated moth, one of 12 currently accepted *Bombyx* species in Catalogue of Life

The silkworm is the caterpillar of a moth domesticated from the wild *Bombyx
mandarina* lineage more than five thousand years ago. People repeatedly chose
larvae that grew well and spun useful cocoons, eventually producing an animal
that depends heavily on human care; the adult domestic moth has lost effective
flight. A cocoon is built from a long fibroin filament bonded with sericin, so
the shelter made for metamorphosis can be softened and reeled into thread.
“Silkworm breeds” differ in colour, development and cocoon properties, but they
are lines within *B. mori*. This is domestication written into an insect's whole
life cycle, not a separate species for every silk.

## The Kept: companion animals

### Mutt

*Canis familiaris* · a mixed-ancestry domestic dog derived from *Canis lupus*; one of 8 living *Canis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

“Mutt” is an ancestry description, not a breed and certainly not a species.
Many are not tidy first-generation crosses between two named breeds: in one
large genomic study, most mutts carried meaningful ancestry from four or more
breeds, and no single breed dominated many of them. Their bodies and behaviour
are therefore mosaics, but even a DNA percentage is not a personality test.
Another broad study found breed ancestry explained only a modest share of
behavioural variation among individual dogs. Meet the animal in front of you —
its health, learning and lived experience — before inventing a character from
an uncertain family tree.

### Pit Bull

*Canis familiaris* · a domestic dog type label, not one species or universally defined breed; one of 8 living *Canis* taxa in the Mammal Diversity Database

“Pit bull” changes meaning with speaker and registry. It may indicate the
American Pit Bull Terrier, group several bull-and-terrier breeds together, or
be applied to a mixed dog with a broad head and muscular build. That looseness
has consequences: shelter studies found that observers often disagreed over
which dogs were pit-bull type, and visual labels did not consistently match
tested ancestry. History can shape tendencies in a breeding population, but a
type name cannot safely predict one companion's behaviour. Humane training,
socialisation, supervision and assessment of the individual matter for every
dog. The photograph records a dog; the contested label should not be allowed to
write its fate.

### Corn Snake

*Pantherophis guttatus* · one of 9 living *Pantherophis* species

One of nine living *Pantherophis* snakes, the Corn Snake is a nonvenomous
constrictor native to the southeastern United States. Its common name may come
from finding it around corn stores rich in mice, or from the maize-like black-
and-white checks across its belly; the origin is not settled. The wild animal's
orange saddles break up its outline through leaf litter, while captive breeders
have produced many colour and pattern morphs. Those morphs remain this species.
Its reputation as a manageable companion should not erase the animal behind it:
a climbing, hiding, heat-dependent predator whose long-term care must be built
around snake biology rather than convenience.

### Bearded Dragon

*Pogona vitticeps* · Central Bearded Dragon, one of 6 living *Pogona* species

One of six living *Pogona* species, the Central Bearded Dragon communicates
with a body that can look almost theatrical. It expands and darkens the spiny
skin beneath its throat, bobs its head and may wave one forelimb in social
displays. Temperature reaches deeper than basking behaviour. In this species,
high incubation temperatures can override the usual ZZ genetic pathway and
produce functional females from genetically male embryos — a striking link
between climate and development. Captive colours and a calm-looking pose do not
make it a domestic breed. It remains an Australian lizard requiring ultraviolet
light, heat gradients and room to perform the behaviours its signals evolved
to serve.

### Russian Tortoise

*Testudo horsfieldii* · Central Asian or four-toed tortoise, one of 5 living *Testudo* species

One of five living *Testudo* species, this tortoise ranges across Central Asia;
the pet-trade name “Russian” tells only part of its geography. Four toes on each
forefoot give it another common name, while strong limbs make it an exceptional
digger. In the continental wild it escapes punishing heat and cold underground
and may fit much of its feeding and breeding into a brief active season. A
small shell therefore does not mean a small spatial life. It can tunnel under a
carelessly built boundary and may live for decades, turning an impulse purchase
into a commitment that outlasts homes, jobs and sometimes the person who first
carried it home.

### Leopard Gecko

*Eublepharis macularius* · one of 8 living *Eublepharis* species

One of eight living *Eublepharis* species, the Leopard Gecko keeps features
that many familiar wall geckos have lost: movable eyelids and toes without
adhesive climbing pads. It walks rather than sticking to glass. Its thick tail
stores fat and can be shed at a fracture plane during danger; a replacement
grows, but it is not an exact copy and the escape spends valuable reserves.
Captive breeders have produced pale, orange and patternless morphs, all still
*E. macularius*. Gentle handling can make an individual seem domesticated, yet
its nocturnal rhythm, thermal choices and need for secure retreats remain those
of a small ground-dwelling predator.

## The Vigil: extinct in recent history

### Alaotra Grebe

*Tachybaptus rufolavatus* · an extinct sixth member beside 5 living *Tachybaptus* species

This small grebe lived only around Lake Alaotra in Madagascar. Short wings
limited its power to abandon a failing wetland, while introduced predatory fish,
monofilament gillnets and heavy change to the lake pressed on the population
together. The last generally accepted records came in the 1980s; after repeated
searches failed, it was declared extinct in 2010. No single final bird or final
cause can be named with confidence. That is the warning in the Vigil: an animal
can disappear through several ordinary pressures, each survivable in isolation,
until an endemic species with nowhere else to go is represented only by skins,
notes and disputed sightings.

### Bachman's Warbler

*Vermivora bachmanii* · declared extinct by the U.S. in 2023; AviList retains it as Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct) beside 2 living *Vermivora* species

Bachman's Warbler migrated between swampy forests and cane-rich understory in
the southeastern United States and winter habitat in Cuba. Clearing those
bottomland woods removed a specialised breeding landscape over a huge area.
Reports continued after the last widely accepted United States record in 1962,
and possible Cuban observations reached the 1980s, but none produced the
evidence needed to demonstrate survival. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service
removed it from Endangered Species Act protection as extinct in 2023. Its
silence is not proof that migration failed in one dramatic moment; it is the
end point of lost habitat at both ends of a journey and decades without a
verifiable return. AviList and the IUCN still retain it as Critically Endangered
(Possibly Extinct), preserving an honest disagreement between authorities.

### Christmas Island Forest Skink

*Emoia nativitatis* · one of 78 *Emoia* species returned by the Reptile Database; extinct

This small skink existed only on Christmas Island. It was still described as
common in the late twentieth century, then collapsed so quickly that the last
wild record came in 2010. A captive-rescue attempt began with only a few
animals; the final known individual, nicknamed Gump, died in 2014, making this
Australia's first recorded reptile extinction since European colonisation. The
proximate cause remains unresolved. Introduced predators, including the wolf
snake and giant centipede, and disease have all been investigated, but certainty
arrived too late for the animal. The honest memorial preserves that uncertainty:
knowing a loss occurred is not the same as knowing which pressure dealt the
last blow.

### Round Island Burrowing Boa

*Bolyeria multocarinata* · the only named species in *Bolyeria*; extinct

The only species placed in *Bolyeria* survived on tiny Round Island off
Mauritius after vanishing from the larger islands. It belonged to a singular
boa family whose upper jaw contains an extra joint, a feeding mechanism shared
today only by the surviving Round Island keel-scaled boa. Goats and rabbits
stripped vegetation and accelerated erosion; introduced predators may have
added pressure. The last confirmed burrowing boa was found in 1975. Round
Island's habitat has since been restored with remarkable success, but this
lineage cannot return with the plants. Recovery can rescue a community while
still arriving one species too late.

### Tecopa Pupfish

*Cyprinodon nevadensis calidae* · an extinct subspecies of the Amargosa Pupfish, within a genus of 49 FishBase-listed species

The Tecopa Pupfish was a subspecies, not a separate species: a tiny local form
of *Cyprinodon nevadensis* adapted to the hot, salty outflows of springs at
Tecopa, California. Fish and Wildlife Service records describe animals enduring
water near 43 °C. Bathhouse development joined spring channels and altered
their flow, while introduced fishes and hybridisation further erased the
isolated population. Surveys failed to find it, and in 1981 it became the first
animal removed from the U.S. endangered list because it was judged extinct.
Its loss shows why rank is no measure of value: a subspecies can carry an
adaptation found nowhere else.

## Research notes

### The Floe

- **Taxonomic baseline (birds):** AviList v2025b is the latest released global checklist as of 1 September 2026. It retains Adélie and gentoo in a 3-species *Pygoscelis*, king penguin in a 2-species *Aptenodytes*, Arctic tern in a 13-species *Sterna*, and all 7 skuas/jaegers in *Stercorarius*. It also supports the monotypic bird genera used above. [AviList v2025b checklist and downloads](https://www.avilist.org/checklist/v2025b/) and [AviList 2026 update note](https://www.avilist.org/avilist-updates-june-2026/).
- **Taxonomic baseline (mammals):** Mammal Diversity Database v2.5 recognises 8 living *Canis*, 7 living *Dicrostonyx*, and 4 living *Pusa*. The fourth *Pusa* is *P. saimensis*, split from *P. hispida* following 2025 work. MDD treats Arctic wolf within *Canis lupus*; use of *C. l. arctos* is a subspecific convention rather than a separate MDD species. [MDD taxonomy table](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/explore/taxonomy-table/), [gray wolf](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1005943/), [collared lemming](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1002125/), [ringed seal](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1005928/), and [Saimaa seal](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1006964/).
- **Taxonomic baseline (fishes and sea spider):** FishBase genus searches currently list 51 *Salvelinus*, three *Pagetodes*, and only one living species each in *Boreogadus* and *Reinhardtius*. WoRMS currently accepts two named *Mallotus*, 74 *Colossendeis*, and the two named *Dissostichus* species. Counts are database snapshots, not claims that future revisions cannot change them. [FishBase *Salvelinus* list](https://www.fishbase.se/identification/SpeciesList.php?genus=salvelinus), [FishBase *Pagetodes* list](https://fishbase.se/identification/SpeciesList.php?genus=Pagetodes), [WoRMS *Mallotus* list](https://www.marinespecies.org/aphia.php?p=taxlist&tName=Mallotus), [WoRMS *Colossendeis* list](https://www.marinespecies.org/aphia.php?p=taxlist&tName=Colossendeis), and [WoRMS *Dissostichus mawsoni*](https://www.marinespecies.org/aphia.php?id=234836&p=taxdetails).
- **Adélie Penguin:** Australian Antarctic Program describes pebble nests, pebble stealing, nest drainage, pack-ice wintering, diet, and tobogganing. [Adélie penguin profile](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/animals/penguins/adelie-penguin/).
- **Antarctic Icefish:** CCAMLR's Ross Sea field guide applies the exact familiar name to *Cryodraco antarcticus*. Current FishBase, WoRMS, NCBI and recent genomic work accept the combination *Pagetodes antarcticus*. FishBase documents the 90–600 m depth range, Southern Ocean distribution, fish-and-krill diet and long pelagic larval phase; haemoglobin loss is shared by the whole icefish family. [CCAMLR Ross Sea fish guide](https://www.ccamlr.org/en/system/files/AEBR_134_2032_Ross-Sea-fishes-guide%20UPDATED%2018%20Feb%202015.pdf), [FishBase species account](https://fishbase.se/summary/Pagetodes_antarcticus.html), and [icefish haemoglobin evolution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10651078/).
- **Capelin:** the entry fixes the broad familiar name to Atlantic Capelin, *Mallotus villosus*. WoRMS accepts the Pacific *M. catervarius* as a second named species, while Canadian fisheries work reports a third, still-unnamed Atlantic lineage. [DFO capelin biology and management](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/fisheries-peches/ifmp-gmp/capelin-area12-16-zone-capelan/2020/index-eng.html).
- **Antarctic Sea Spider:** Genomic and mitochondrial studies treat *C. megalonyx* as a species complex, with evidence for several lineages and some hybridisation; physiological work documents a gut extending into the legs and oxygen exchange across porous cuticle, while direct observations record predation on *Clione antarctica*. [Polar Biology genomic study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00300-020-02636-z), [mitochondrial/hybridisation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4632570/), [respiration and anatomy study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6501676/), and [feeding study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ivb.12210).
- **Antarctic Toothfish:** CCAMLR summarises slow growth, late maturity, low fecundity, diet, and ecology; genomic work covers antifreeze glycoproteins, lipid storage, and reduced bone mineralisation associated with buoyancy. [CCAMLR species description](https://fishdocs.ccamlr.org/SpDescr_TOA_2020.html) and [toothfish genome study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6457430/).
- **Arctic Char and Arctic Cod:** Fisheries and Oceans Canada documents resident and sea-run char, their short marine feeding season, and obligatory freshwater wintering. NOAA and a primary genetics paper support Arctic cod’s food-web role and independently evolved antifreeze system. [DFO Arctic char profile](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/species-especes/profiles-profils/arctic-char-omble-chevalier-eng.html), [NOAA Arctic cod research](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/resource/document/arctic-cod-boreogadus-saida-age-and-growth-research), and [Arctic cod antifreeze study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29510906/).
- **Arctic Tern:** Geolocator studies measured migrations above 80,000 km and showed looping routes linked to winds and productive waters; the prose deliberately avoids claiming one fixed route or an unqualified world record. [PNAS tracking study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2836663/) and [later multi-ocean tracking study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ece3.5459).
- **Arctic Wolf:** A 2023 methods paper reports the single collared Ellesmere male, its approximately 6,640 km² range, pack size, and prey. Because this is one animal, the entry presents it as an example, not a species-wide average. [GPS cluster study](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2688-8319.12204).
- **Capelin:** DFO describes boom-and-bust abundance, beach rolling, adhesive eggs, spawning cues, high post-spawning mortality, and the species’ keystone forage role. [Atlantic capelin profile](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/species-especes/profiles-profils/capelin-capelan-eng.html).
- **Collared Lemming:** Alaska Department of Fish and Game describes the white winter moult and bifid digging claws; MDD records unresolved inclusion of *vinogradovi*. [ADFG collared lemming profile](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=northerncollaredlemming.printerfriendly) and [MDD taxon page](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1002125/).
- **Gentoo Penguin:** Australian Antarctic Program supports the nesting and breeding account. A 2020 paper proposed four species, and an April 2026 integrative study again proposed a four-species revision, but AviList v2025b has not yet incorporated it. This is the most actively moving bird taxonomy in the set. [Gentoo profile](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/animals/penguins/gentoo-penguin/), [2020 taxonomic study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7771148/), and [2026 integrative study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13264629/).
- **Greenland Halibut:** DFO documents metamorphosis and incomplete eye migration, broad peripheral vision, deep habitat, and daily vertical movement, including the estimate of roughly 25 percent of time in the water column. [DFO management plan biology section](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/fisheries-peches/ifmp-gmp/groundfish-poisson-fond/2020/halibut-fletan-eng.htm).
- **Ivory Gull:** Canada’s 2023 assessment documents pack-ice dependence, scavenging of polar-bear kills, polynya use, and satellite-tag results showing about 90 percent of winter over sea ice. [COSEWIC Ivory Gull report](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/ivory-gull-2023.html).
- **King Penguin:** Australian Antarctic Program gives the 13–16 month breeding cycle, egg-on-feet incubation, alternate-year success, colony recognition, and lanternfish-heavy diet. [King penguin profile](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/animals/penguins/king-penguin/).
- **Little Auk:** Norwegian Polar Institute documents copepod feeding, throat-pouch provisioning, scree nesting, immense colonies, and the estimate of about 100 tonnes of guano from 50,000 pairs. [Little auk profile](https://npolar.no/en/species/little-auk/).
- **Long-tailed Duck:** Cornell Lab reports dives to roughly 200 feet (about 60 m), wing-propelled feeding, the high submerged-to-surface ratio, and unusual moult timing. [Cornell species account](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/guide/Long-tailed_Duck/overview).
- **Ringed Seal:** NOAA documents foreclaw-maintained holes through 6 feet or more of ice, snow lairs, pupping, diet, and the seal’s importance to polar bears. [NOAA Ringed Seal](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/ringed-seal) and [NOAA science page](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/ringed-seal/science).
- **Rock Ptarmigan:** Alaska Department of Fish and Game covers seasonal white/brown plumage, feathered feet, long digging nails, and rapid construction of insulating snow burrows. [ADFG species profile](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=rockptarmigan.main) and [ADFG winter adaptation article](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=wildlifenews.view_article&articles_id=855).
- **Ross’s Gull:** A two-bird satellite study measured minimum journeys of 1,777 and 8,254 km and documented use of 80–85°N sea ice; Canadian assessment work shows that different breeding populations may winter in different oceans. [Satellite-tracking study, DOI 10.1007/s10336-015-1273-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10336-015-1273-7) and [COSEWIC Ross’s Gull report](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/ross-gull-2021.html).
- **Snow Petrel:** Australian Antarctic Program documents pack-ice association, crevice nesting, diet, and two size-based subspecies; its research magazine reports colonies up to roughly 440 km inland. Mixed pairing between size forms argues against presenting them as settled separate species. [Snow petrel profile](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/animals/flying-birds/petrels-and-shearwaters/snow-petrel/), [inland colony research](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/magazine/issue-36-june-2019/science/fossilised-spit-reveals-3600-years-of-snow-petrel-colonisation/), and [mate-choice study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347201917130).
- **South Polar Skua:** Australian Antarctic Program supports nesting, diet, and breeding timing; BTO summarises tracking evidence that some individuals reach the North Atlantic. The Australian page uses the older combination *Catharacta maccormicki*, while AviList uses *Stercorarius maccormicki*. [South polar skua profile](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/animals/flying-birds/south-polar-skua/) and [BTO species account](https://www.bto.org/learn/about-birds/birdfacts/south-polar-skua).

  v2.5, released 2026-07-28. Its current table gives two *Rupicapra*, seven
  *Ovis*, nine *Equus* (including domestic horse and donkey), one *Oreamnos*, one
  *Procavia*, one *Pantholops*, three *Eupetaurus*, four *Oryx* and four *Lynx*.

  *Boreorana sylvatica* among alternate treatments of the Wood Frog.
- **Spider counts:** the World Spider Catalog v27 lists one hundred accepted
  *Euophrys* species and eight accepted *Brachypelma* species. *B. hamorii* is
  the intended Mexican Redknee; older uses of *B. smithi* often referred to it.
- **Apollo count:** literature commonly describes about fifty *Parnassius*
  species, while warning that species-versus-subspecies boundaries are disputed.
- **Woolly Flying Squirrel:** the broad card name is retained, but the exact
  species is the Western Woolly Flying Squirrel. Two additional living species
  were recognised in the 2021 revision.
- **Little Blue Penguin:** AviList recognises one *Eudyptula* species and treats
  *novaehollandiae* as a subspecies. The entry records the published two-species
  alternative rather than presenting either treatment as timeless.
- **Later caveat check:** the Argali entry deliberately avoids enumerating
  subspecies, and the Apollo entry avoids assigning the many proposed forms a
  definitive rank. Both complexes merit ordinary claim-by-claim review before
  final verification.

### The Breeding Centre, The Kept, and The Vigil

- **Domestic mammals:** The Mammal Diversity Database treats the dog as
  *Canis familiaris*, a domestic form of *C. lupus*, and the cat as *Felis
  catus*, a domestic form of *F. lybica*. Its current taxonomy table returns
  eight living *Canis* listings, seven *Felis*, twelve *Vulpes* and two
  *Oryctolagus*. Other authorities may format dog and cat as subspecies; the
  prose states the wild parent so rank choice cannot turn a breed into a
  species.
- **Breed and form labels:** Angora Rabbit is a wool-breed group; Ball Python
  Morph is a colour or pattern variant; Fancy Pigeon is a broad fancier's
  category; Domestic Fox refers here to the long-running tame-selection lines.
  Mutt is mixed ancestry, while Pit Bull is a context-dependent type label.
  None is treated as a separate species.
- **Registry dependence:** Cat and rabbit breed boundaries vary by registry.
  The Egyptian Mau paragraph deliberately avoids claiming an unbroken ancient
  Egyptian pedigree. Munchkin recognition and welfare remain contested. These
  are later-review caveats, not omissions to be silently filled with breed
  folklore.
- **Genus counts:** Reptile Database exact-genus searches on 2026-09-01 returned
  10 *Python*, 9 *Pantherophis*, 6 *Pogona*, 5 *Testudo*, 8 *Eublepharis* and
  78 *Emoia*. Amphibian Species of the World currently accepts twenty-six
  living *Ambystoma* species.
  Catalogue of Life's 2026-09-01 tree exposes twelve accepted *Bombyx* species;
  a 2015 specialist revision recognised six, so that insect count is especially
  sensitive to source and date.
- **Recently extinct animals:** The *Tachybaptus* wording follows a list with
  five living species plus the Alaotra Grebe; lists that do not split the
  Tricolored Grebe will show one fewer. *Vermivora* has two living warblers plus
  Bachman's. The Christmas Island Forest Skink's immediate cause remains
  unresolved. The Tecopa Pupfish was an extinct subspecies, and FishBase
  currently lists 49 *Cyprinodon* species; neither fact diminishes its loss.
